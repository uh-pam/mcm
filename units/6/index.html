This unit uncovers the deepest structure a square matrix carries: its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. A matrix transformation usually sends a vector to a new length and a new direction, but along a few special directions it does something far simpler—it merely stretches, leaving the direction unchanged. Those directions are the eigenvectors, the stretch factors are the eigenvalues, and together they are the natural axes of the matrix. We find them from a determinant equation, use them to diagonalise a matrix and so compute its powers cheaply, and finally watch them govern the long-run behaviour of a system evolving in steps—a Markov chain.

Where this fits. Eigensystems gather up the whole linear-algebra strand. The eigenvalues are roots of a determinant equation, each eigenvector is found by solving a homogeneous system with Gaussian elimination, and diagonalisation is built from an inverse—all three from Unit 5; the eigenvectors are the invariant directions of its transformations, drawn with the vectors of Unit 4, and an eigenvalue may even be complex, the numbers of Unit 3. This is where the linear-algebra strand ends, but not where the ideas do: mathematics and data-science students meet eigenvalues again in a dedicated linear-algebra course—they underlie principal-component analysis and much of modern data science—while physicists meet them as the normal modes of a vibrating system and the observables of quantum mechanics.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The idea: directions a matrix preserves

A transformation generally turns a vector to a new direction. But for most matrices there are a few special directions that are not turned—along them the matrix only stretches or shrinks. Such a direction is an eigenvector, and its stretch factor is the eigenvalue. A pure stretch leaves the coordinate axes pointing as they were, a reflection fixes its mirror line, and a plane rotation through any angle other than 000 or π\piπ—as we shall see—fixes no real direction at all.

Let A\mathbf{A}A be a square matrix. A non-zero vector v\mathbf{v}v is an eigenvector of A\mathbf{A}A, with eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, if Av=λv.\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}.Av=λv. The eigenvector marks a direction the transformation preserves; the eigenvalue is the factor by which it stretches (λ>1\lambda>1λ>1), shrinks (0<λ<10<\lambda<10<λ<1), or flips and scales (λ<0\lambda<0λ<0) along that direction. The zero vector is excluded, since A0=λ0\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0}=\lambda\mathbf{0}A0=λ0 holds for every λ\lambdaλ and says nothing.

Complex eigenvalues

A real matrix need not have real eigenvalues—and geometrically the reason is rotation, which leaves no real direction fixed.

The quarter-turn R=(0−110)\mathbf{R}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}R=(01​−10​) turns every vector by 90∘90^\circ90∘, so it should fix no real direction. Its characteristic equation confirms this: det⁡(−λ−11−λ)=λ2+1=0 ⟹ λ=± i.\det\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda & -1\\ 1 & -\lambda\end{pmatrix}=\lambda^2+1=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda=\pm\,\mathrm{i}.det(−λ1​−1−λ​)=λ2+1=0 ⟹ λ=±i. Complex eigenvalues are the algebraic signature of rotation: whenever a system spirals, complex eigenvalues are at work, their modulus setting the growth or decay and their argument the frequency. (Oscillation alone is weaker evidence—a real eigenvalue λ<0\lambda<0λ<0 flips the sign at every step, as the swap matrix of Section 3 will show.)

Symmetric and Hermitian matrices

Two families of matrices have especially well-behaved eigensystems, and both are central to applications. A real symmetric matrix (AT=A\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{A}AT=A) has eigenvalues that are all real and eigenvectors that, for distinct eigenvalues, are orthogonal. The 3×33\times33×3 matrix of Example 1.2 is symmetric, and indeed its eigenvalues 1,2,41,2,41,2,4 came out real and its eigenvectors mutually orthogonal—no coincidence, but a guarantee.

The complex counterpart is a Hermitian matrix.

A complex square matrix is Hermitian if it equals its own conjugate transpose, A†=A,A†:=A‾T,\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{A},\qquad \mathbf{A}^{\dagger}:=\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}},A†=A,A†:=AT, that is, transposing and conjugating every entry (Unit 3) returns A\mathbf{A}A. The diagonal entries are therefore real, and entries across the diagonal are conjugates. Like symmetric matrices, Hermitian matrices have only real eigenvalues.

Real eigenvalues are exactly why quantum mechanics represents every measurable quantity by a Hermitian operator: the eigenvalues are the possible measured values. The orthogonality of the eigenvectors—real or complex—has a powerful consequence for diagonalisation, taken up in Section 2.3, and is the foundation of principal-component analysis, where the symmetric covariance matrix of a data set is split into orthogonal directions of greatest variance.

Diagonalisation and matrix powers

When a matrix has a full set of eigenvectors they make the best possible coordinate system: in eigenvector coordinates the matrix is simply a list of scale factors. This is diagonalisation, and it makes otherwise heavy computations—above all, powers of a matrix—almost free.

Diagonalisation

A square matrix A\mathbf{A}A is diagonalisable if it can be written A=PDP−1,\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1},A=PDP−1, with D\mathbf{D}D diagonal and P\mathbf{P}P invertible. (Equivalently P−1AP=D\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P} =\mathbf{D}P−1AP=D: the matrices A\mathbf{A}A and D\mathbf{D}D are similar, describing the same map in different coordinates.)

The eigenvectors and eigenvalues are exactly the ingredients. If P=[ v1 ⋯ vn ]\mathbf{P}=[\,\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n\,]P=[v1​ ⋯ vn​] has the eigenvectors as its columns and D=diag⁡(λ1,…,λn)\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)D=diag(λ1​,…,λn​) the eigenvalues, then column by column AP=[ λ1v1 ⋯ λnvn ]=PD\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}=[\,\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \lambda_n\mathbf{v}_n\,]=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}AP=[λ1​v1​ ⋯ λn​vn​]=PD, so A=PDP−1\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}A=PDP−1 provided P\mathbf{P}P is invertible.

An n×nn\times nn×n matrix is diagonalisable if and only if it has nnn linearly independent eigenvectors; these form the columns of P\mathbf{P}P and the corresponding eigenvalues the diagonal of D\mathbf{D}D. In particular, a matrix with nnn distinct eigenvalues is always diagonalisable, since eigenvectors from different eigenvalues are independent.

The last clause is the one part the theorem leaned on without justifying; it is short to prove, and worth proving, since it is what makes distinct eigenvalues enough.

Symmetric matrices and orthogonal diagonalisation

For a symmetric matrix the eigenvectors are not merely independent but orthogonal (§1.4), and that makes its diagonalisation especially clean. Scaling each eigenvector to unit length gives an orthonormal set, so the eigenvector matrix becomes an orthogonal matrix Q\mathbf{Q}Q, one whose inverse is simply its transpose, Q−1=QT\mathbf{Q}^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}Q−1=QT.

Every real symmetric matrix can be written A=QDQT,\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}},A=QDQT, with D\mathbf{D}D diagonal (the real eigenvalues) and Q\mathbf{Q}Q orthogonal (orthonormal eigenvectors as columns). In particular a symmetric matrix is always diagonalisable. (Eigenvectors from distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal; within a repeated eigenvalue’s eigenspace an orthonormal basis is chosen, e.g. by Gram–Schmidt.)

The symmetric matrix A=(210131012)\mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1&0\\1&3&1\\0&1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)A=(210​131​012​) has eigenvalues 1,2,41,2,41,2,4 and the mutually orthogonal eigenvectors found earlier. When diagonalisation fails

Not every matrix is diagonalisable. The obstruction is always a repeated eigenvalue that fails to supply enough eigenvectors.

The matrix A=(2102)\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}A=(20​12​) has characteristic equation (2−λ)2=0(2-\lambda)^2=0(2−λ)2=0, so λ=2\lambda=2λ=2 is a double root. But solving (A−2I)v=0(\mathbf{A}-2\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}(A−2I)v=0, (0100)v=0 ⟹ v2=0,\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0} \ \Longrightarrow\ v_2=0,(00​10​)v=0 ⟹ v2​=0, leaves only the single eigen-direction (1,0)T(1,0)^{\mathsf{T}}(1,0)T. There is one independent eigenvector where two are needed, so A\mathbf{A}A cannot be diagonalised.

For an eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, its algebraic multiplicity is the number of times it repeats as a root of the characteristic polynomial, and its geometric multiplicity is the dimension of its eigenspace—the number of independent eigenvectors it provides. The geometric multiplicity never exceeds the algebraic one, and A is diagonalisable  ⟺  geometric=algebraic for every eigenvalue.\mathbf{A}\text{ is diagonalisable}\iff \text{geometric}=\text{algebraic for \emph{every} eigenvalue.}A is diagonalisable⟺geometric=algebraic for every eigenvalue.

In the defective example the eigenvalue 222 has algebraic multiplicity 222 but geometric multiplicity 111, and the shortfall is exactly what blocks diagonalisation. A repeated eigenvalue is therefore harmless when its eigenspace is as large as its repeat count—the symmetric matrices of Section 2.3, for instance, are never defective—and distinct eigenvalues avoid the question entirely, each forcing one eigenvector. When an eigenspace does fall short, the missing directions are filled by generalised eigenvectors, and the nearest-to-diagonal form a matrix can then reach is the Jordan canonical form—both developed in the Linear Algebra module.

Application: dynamical systems and Markov chains

Eigenvalues come into their own when a system evolves by repeated multiplication. If a state vector updates each step by xn+1=Axn\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_nxn+1​=Axn​, then after nnn steps xn=Anx0\mathbf{x}_n=\mathbf{A}^n\mathbf{x}_0xn​=Anx0​—and the diagonalisation of Section 2 turns the long-run behaviour into a question about the eigenvalues alone.

Markov chains

A Markov chain models a system that moves between a fixed set of states with given probabilities. This dominant-eigenvalue principle is everywhere. Google’s original PageRank ranks web pages by the steady-state eigenvector of a giant transition matrix of links; a population’s long-term age profile is the dominant eigenvector of its growth matrix; and the stability of an equilibrium in physics or economics is decided by whether the governing eigenvalues have modulus below one. The eigenvector is the destination, and the dominant eigenvalue is the speed.

Self-check

Six questions on the ideas this unit is most often misunderstood on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

A 2×22\times22×2 matrix has the repeated eigenvalue λ=4\lambda=4λ=4. Is it diagonalisable?

A repeated eigenvalue is a warning, not a verdict. What matters is the geometric multiplicity — the number of independent eigenvectors, which is the nullity of A−4I\mathbf{A}-4\mathbf{I}A−4I. If that is 222 the matrix is diagonalisable (indeed A=4I\mathbf{A}=4\mathbf{I}A=4I); if it is 111 the matrix is defective. Invertibility is irrelevant: (4104)\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\0&4\end{pmatrix}(40​14​) is invertible and not diagonalisable.

The rotation R=(0−110)\mathbf{R}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}R=(01​−10​) has eigenvalues ±i\pm\mathrm{i}±i. What does having no real eigenvalue mean geometrically?

A real eigenvector would be a direction the map merely stretches. A quarter-turn moves every direction, so no such real direction exists — which is exactly what a complex-conjugate pair of eigenvalues records. The matrix is not singular (det⁡R=1\det\mathbf{R}=1detR=1), and it does have eigenvectors: they simply live in C2\mathbb{C}^2C2 rather than R2\mathbb{R}^2R2.

Why is v=0\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}v=0 excluded from the definition of an eigenvector?

A0=0=λ0\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}=\lambda\mathbf{0}A0=0=λ0 is true for every scalar λ\lambdaλ whatsoever. Admitting 0\mathbf{0}0 would make every number an eigenvalue of every matrix, and the definition would carry no information. Note that eigenvalues themselves may perfectly well be zero — that happens exactly when A\mathbf{A}A is singular.

You compute eigenvalues λ1=3, λ2=5\lambda_1=3,\ \lambda_2=5λ1​=3, λ2​=5 for a matrix with trace 999. What should you conclude?

For an n×nn\times nn×n matrix the eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant. Here 3+5=8≠93+5=8\neq93+5=8=9, so something has gone wrong — most often a sign slip in expanding det⁡(A−λI)\det(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})det(A−λI). This is the cheapest check available and is worth doing every single time.

In a diagonalisation A=PDP−1\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}A=PDP−1, what must be true of the order of the columns of P\mathbf{P}P?

Any ordering of the eigenvectors gives a valid diagonalisation, but the kkk-th column of P\mathbf{P}P must be an eigenvector for the kkk-th diagonal entry of D\mathbf{D}D. Swapping one without the other breaks the identity. Normalising is optional for diagonalisation in general — it matters for the orthogonal diagonalisation of a symmetric matrix, where P−1=PT\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\mathbf{P}^{\mathsf{T}}P−1=PT requires orthonormal columns.

A Markov chain's transition matrix always has λ=1\lambda=1λ=1 as an eigenvalue. What does the corresponding eigenvector represent?

Pv=1⋅v\mathbf{P}\mathbf{v}=1\cdot\mathbf{v}Pv=1⋅v says precisely that applying one step leaves v\mathbf{v}v unchanged, which is what a steady state is. Scaled so its entries sum to 111, it is the long-run distribution. Nothing grows without bound: the other eigenvalues have modulus below 111 for a regular chain, so their contributions die away and the λ=1\lambda=1λ=1 direction is what survives.
