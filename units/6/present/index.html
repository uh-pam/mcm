Eigensystems

By the end of this unit you will be able to

  • Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors of 2×22\times22×2 and 3×33\times33×3 matrices, with a trace/determinant check.
  • Interpret complex eigenvalues as rotation, and the eigenstructure of symmetric and Hermitian matrices.
  • Decide diagonalisability and use diagonalisation to compute matrix powers.
  • Apply orthogonal diagonalisation and analyse a Markov chain via its dominant eigenvalue.

A matrix usually sends a vector to a new length and a new direction, but along a few special directions it merely stretches (or flips), leaving the line through the vector unchanged. Those directions are the eigenvectors, the stretch factors are the eigenvalues, and together they are the natural axes of the matrix. We find them from a determinant equation, use them to diagonalise a matrix and so compute its powers cheaply, and watch them govern the long-run behaviour of a Markov chain.

Where this fits. The linear-algebra strand ends here, and uses all of Unit 5: eigenvalues are roots of a determinant equation, eigenvectors solve a homogeneous system (right-hand side zero) by Gaussian elimination, and diagonalisation needs an inverse. Eigenvectors are the invariant directions of Unit 5’s transformations, drawn with the vectors of Unit 4, and an eigenvalue may be complex (Units 2–3).

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

The idea: directions a matrix preserves

A transformation generally turns a vector to a new direction. But for most matrices there are a few special lines through the origin that are not turned—along them the matrix only stretches, shrinks or flips. Such a direction is an eigenvector, and its stretch factor is the eigenvalue. A pure stretch leaves the coordinate axes pointing as they were, a reflection fixes its mirror line, and a plane rotation through any angle other than 000 or π\piπ—as we shall see—fixes no real direction at all.

Let A\mathbf{A}A be a square matrix. A non-zero vector v\mathbf{v}v is an eigenvector of A\mathbf{A}A, with eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, if Av=λv.\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}.Av=λv. The eigenvector marks a direction the transformation preserves; the eigenvalue is the factor by which it stretches (λ>1\lambda>1λ>1), shrinks (0<λ<10<\lambda<10<λ<1), or flips and scales (λ<0\lambda<0λ<0) along that direction. The zero vector is excluded, since A0=λ0\mathbf{A}\mathbf{0}=\lambda\mathbf{0}A0=λ0 holds for every λ\lambdaλ and says nothing.

Figure 1 shows the matrix of the running example below acting on one of its eigenvectors and on an ordinary vector. Eigenvectors expose the skeleton of a transformation: once we know the directions it merely scales, and by how much, we know almost everything about how it acts.

Finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors

To find eigenvalues we turn Av=λv\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}Av=λv into a condition with no v\mathbf{v}v in it. Complex eigenvalues

A real matrix need not have real eigenvalues—and geometrically the reason is rotation, which leaves no real direction fixed.

The quarter-turn R=(0−110)\mathbf{R}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}R=(01​−10​) turns every vector by 90∘90^\circ90∘, so it should fix no real direction. Its characteristic equation confirms this: det⁡(−λ−11−λ)=λ2+1=0 ⟹ λ=± i.\det\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda & -1\\ 1 & -\lambda\end{pmatrix}=\lambda^2+1=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda=\pm\,\mathrm{i}.det(−λ1​−1−λ​)=λ2+1=0 ⟹ λ=±i. The eigenvalues are the imaginary numbers ±i\pm\mathrm{i}±i of Unit 2, with complex eigenvectors (1,−i)T(1,-\mathrm{i})^{\mathsf{T}}(1,−i)T for λ=i\lambda=\mathrm{i}λ=i and (1,i)T(1,\mathrm{i})^{\mathsf{T}}(1,i)T for λ=−i\lambda=-\mathrm{i}λ=−i. There are no real eigenvectors, exactly as the geometry demands.

More generally the rotation by angle θ\thetaθ, R(θ)=(cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ)\mathbf{R}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}R(θ)=(cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​), has characteristic equation λ2−2cos⁡θ λ+1=0\lambda^2-2\cos\theta\,\lambda+1=0λ2−2cosθλ+1=0 and hence eigenvalues λ=cos⁡θ±isin⁡θ=e±iθ,\lambda=\cos\theta\pm\mathrm{i}\sin\theta=\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\theta},λ=cosθ±isinθ=e±iθ, the unit-modulus complex numbers of Unit 3. The modulus 111 records that a rotation preserves length, and the argument θ\thetaθ is the angle turned. Complex eigenvalues are the algebraic signature of rotation: whenever a system spirals, complex eigenvalues are at work, their modulus setting the growth or decay per step and their argument the turning per step. For a scaled rotation (a−bba)\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)(ab​−ba​) this holds exactly at every step; for any other real 2×22\times22×2 with complex eigenvalues it holds on average over many steps. (Oscillation alone is weaker evidence—a real eigenvalue λ<0\lambda<0λ<0 flips the sign at every step, as the swap matrix of Section 3 will show.)

Symmetric and Hermitian matrices

Two families of matrices have especially well-behaved eigensystems, and both are central to applications. A real symmetric matrix (AT=A\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathbf{A}AT=A) has eigenvalues that are all real and eigenvectors that, for distinct eigenvalues, are orthogonal. The 3×33\times33×3 matrix of Example 1.2 is symmetric, and indeed its eigenvalues 1,2,41,2,41,2,4 came out real and its eigenvectors mutually orthogonal—no coincidence, but a guarantee.

The complex counterpart is a Hermitian matrix.

A complex square matrix is Hermitian if it equals its own conjugate transpose, A†=A,A†:=A‾T,\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{A},\qquad\allowbreak \mathbf{A}^{\dagger}:=\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{\mathsf{T}},A†=A,A†:=AT, that is, transposing and conjugating every entry (Unit 2) returns A\mathbf{A}A. The diagonal entries are therefore real, and entries across the diagonal are conjugates. Like symmetric matrices, Hermitian matrices have only real eigenvalues.

Real eigenvalues are exactly why quantum mechanics represents every measurable quantity by a Hermitian operator: the eigenvalues are the possible measured values. The orthogonality of the eigenvectors—real or complex—has a powerful consequence for diagonalisation, taken up in Section 2.3, and is the foundation of principal-component analysis, where the symmetric covariance matrix of a data set is split into orthogonal directions of greatest variance.

Diagonalisation and matrix powers

When a matrix has a full set of eigenvectors they make the best possible coordinate system: in eigenvector coordinates the matrix is simply a list of scale factors. This is diagonalisation, and it makes otherwise heavy computations—above all, powers of a matrix—almost free.

Diagonalisation

A square matrix A\mathbf{A}A is diagonalisable if it can be written A=PDP−1,\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1},A=PDP−1, with D\mathbf{D}D diagonal and P\mathbf{P}P invertible. (Equivalently P−1AP=D\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P} =\mathbf{D}P−1AP=D: the matrices A\mathbf{A}A and D\mathbf{D}D are similar, describing the same map in different coordinates.)

The eigenvectors and eigenvalues are exactly the ingredients. If P=[ v1 ⋯ vn ]\mathbf{P}=[\,\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n\,]P=[v1​ ⋯ vn​] has the eigenvectors as its columns and D=diag⁡(λ1,…,λn)\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)D=diag(λ1​,…,λn​) the eigenvalues, then column by column AP=[ λ1v1 ⋯ λnvn ]=PD\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}=[\,\lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \lambda_n\mathbf{v}_n\,]=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}AP=[λ1​v1​ ⋯ λn​vn​]=PD, so A=PDP−1\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1}A=PDP−1 provided P\mathbf{P}P is invertible.

An n×nn\times nn×n matrix is diagonalisable if and only if it has nnn linearly independent eigenvectors; these form the columns of P\mathbf{P}P and the corresponding eigenvalues the diagonal of D\mathbf{D}D. In particular, a matrix with nnn distinct eigenvalues is always diagonalisable, since eigenvectors from different eigenvalues are independent.

The last clause is the one part the theorem leaned on without justifying; it is short to prove, and worth proving, since it is what makes distinct eigenvalues enough.

Computing powers

The point of diagonalising is that powers of a diagonal matrix are trivial—just power the entries—and the change of coordinates passes straight through a power: Ak=(PDP−1)k=PDP−1P⏟ID⋯P−1=PDkP−1,Dk=diag⁡(λ1k,…,λnk).\mathbf{A}^k=(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{P}^{-1})^k =\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}\underbrace{\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{P}}_{\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{D}\cdots\mathbf{P}^{-1} =\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}^k\mathbf{P}^{-1}, \qquad\allowbreak \mathbf{D}^k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\dots,\lambda_n^k).Ak=(PDP−1)k=PDIP−1P​​D⋯P−1=PDkP−1,Dk=diag(λ1k​,…,λnk​). A hundredth power of A\mathbf{A}A, which would otherwise need 999999 matrix multiplications, costs only two—once D100\mathbf{D}^{100}D100 is read off by raising the eigenvalues to the power.

Symmetric matrices and orthogonal diagonalisation

For a symmetric matrix, eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are not merely independent but orthogonal (§1.4), and that makes its diagonalisation especially clean. Scaling each eigenvector to unit length gives an orthonormal set, so the eigenvector matrix becomes an orthogonal matrix Q\mathbf{Q}Q, one whose inverse is simply its transpose, Q−1=QT\mathbf{Q}^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}Q−1=QT.

Every real symmetric matrix can be written A=QDQT,\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf{T}},A=QDQT, with D\mathbf{D}D diagonal (the real eigenvalues) and Q\mathbf{Q}Q orthogonal (orthonormal eigenvectors as columns). In particular a symmetric matrix is always diagonalisable. (Eigenvectors from distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal; within a repeated eigenvalue’s eigenspace—the set of all solutions of (A−λI)v=0(\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}(A−λI)v=0, its eigenvectors together with 0\mathbf{0}0—an orthonormal basis is chosen, e.g. by the Gram–Schmidt process of the Linear Algebra module.)

When diagonalisation fails

Not every matrix is diagonalisable. The obstruction is always a repeated eigenvalue that fails to supply enough eigenvectors.

The matrix A=(2102)\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}A=(20​12​) is triangular, and so is A−λI\mathbf{A}-\lambda\mathbf{I}A−λI, whose determinant is therefore the product of its diagonal (Unit 5): the characteristic equation is (2−λ)2=0(2-\lambda)^2=0(2−λ)2=0. (In the same way the eigenvalues of any triangular matrix are its diagonal entries.) So λ=2\lambda=2λ=2 is a double root. But solving (A−2I)v=0(\mathbf{A}-2\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}(A−2I)v=0, (0100)v=0 ⟹ v2=0,\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0} \ \Longrightarrow\ v_2=0,(00​10​)v=0 ⟹ v2​=0, leaves only the single eigen-direction (1,0)T(1,0)^{\mathsf{T}}(1,0)T. There is one independent eigenvector where two are needed, so A\mathbf{A}A cannot be diagonalised.

For an eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ, its algebraic multiplicity is the number of times it repeats as a root of the characteristic polynomial, and its geometric multiplicity is the number of independent eigenvectors it can supply (the dimension of its eigenspace). The geometric multiplicity never exceeds the algebraic one, and A is diagonalisable  ⟺  geometric=algebraic for every eigenvalue.\mathbf{A}\text{ is diagonalisable}\iff \text{geometric}=\text{algebraic for \emph{every} eigenvalue.}A is diagonalisable⟺geometric=algebraic for every eigenvalue.

In the defective example the eigenvalue 222 has algebraic multiplicity 222 but geometric multiplicity 111, and the shortfall is exactly what blocks diagonalisation. A repeated eigenvalue is therefore harmless when its eigenspace is as large as its repeat count—the symmetric matrices of Section 2.3, for instance, are never defective—and distinct eigenvalues avoid the question entirely, each forcing one eigenvector. When an eigenspace does fall short, the missing directions are filled by generalised eigenvectors, and the nearest-to-diagonal form a matrix can then reach is the Jordan canonical form—both developed in the Linear Algebra module.

Application: dynamical systems and Markov chains

Eigenvalues come into their own when a system evolves by repeated multiplication. If a state vector updates each step by xn+1=Axn\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_nxn+1​=Axn​, then after nnn steps xn=Anx0\mathbf{x}_n=\mathbf{A}^n\mathbf{x}_0xn​=Anx0​—and the diagonalisation of Section 2 turns the long-run behaviour into a question about the eigenvalues alone.

Markov chains

A Markov chain models a system that moves between a fixed set of states with given probabilities. The state vector xn\mathbf{x}_nxn​ holds the probability of being in each state, and a transition matrix P\mathbf{P}P—whose columns are probabilities and so sum to one—advances it one step by xn+1=Pxn\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{x}_nxn+1​=Pxn​. The decay above worked because the second eigenvalue had modulus below 111. In general a Markov chain settles to a unique steady state, independent of the start, if λ=1\lambda=1λ=1 occurs just once and every other eigenvalue is smaller than 111 in modulus. Both halves are needed: the identity matrix I\mathbf{I}I is a perfectly good transition matrix with no eigenvalue outside the unit circle, but λ=1\lambda=1λ=1 is repeated, every distribution is stationary and Inx0=x0\mathbf{I}^n\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{x}_0Inx0​=x0​ never forgets where it started. A chain with a second eigenvalue of modulus 111 need not converge: P=(0110)\mathbf{P}=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)P=(01​10​) has eigenvalues ±1\pm1±1 and simply oscillates forever. The full statement is the Perron–Frobenius theorem of the Linear Algebra module. More broadly, for a general (non-stochastic) system xn+1=Axn\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_nxn+1​=Axn​ the dominant eigenvalue—the one of largest modulus—decides the fate. If every eigenvalue has modulus less than one, every start collapses to zero. If the dominant one has modulus greater than one, the system grows without bound (as for the Fibonacci matrix, with φ>1\varphi>1φ>1), and its eigenvector sets the eventual direction: the other components shrink relative to the dominant one, by the factor ∣λi∣/∣λ1∣\left|\lambda_i\right|/\left|\lambda_1\right|∣λi​∣/∣λ1​∣ per step, though not necessarily in size. Two provisos come with that: the start must have a non-zero component along the dominant eigenvector (with D=(200−1)\mathbf{D}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)D=(20​0−1​) and x0=(0,1)T\mathbf{x}_0=(0,1)^{\mathsf{T}}x0​=(0,1)T the iterates just flip between ±(0,1)T\pm(0,1)^{\mathsf{T}}±(0,1)T, unmoved by λ=2\lambda=2λ=2), and no other eigenvalue may tie it in modulus, or the two modes trade the lead forever instead of one winning.

This dominant-eigenvalue principle is everywhere. Google’s original PageRank ranks web pages by the steady-state eigenvector of a giant transition matrix of links; a population’s long-term age profile is the dominant eigenvector of its growth matrix; and the stability of an equilibrium in a system that evolves in steps, in physics or economics, is decided by whether the governing eigenvalues have modulus below one. With the provisos above, the dominant eigenvalue sets the growth, its eigenvector the eventual direction, and the next-largest modulus how fast the other components fade relative to it.
