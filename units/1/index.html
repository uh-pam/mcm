Numbers, sets and notation

Number systems

Mathematics repeatedly enlarges its number system whenever an operation leads outside the numbers currently available. We begin with the natural numbers used for counting and successively repair each shortcoming.

  • N={ 1,2,3,… }\mathbb{N}= \left\{\,1,2,3,\dots\,\right\}N={1,2,3,…} — the natural numbers (counting). We write N0={ 0,1,2,… }\mathbb{N}_0=\left\{\,0,1,2,\dots\,\right\}N0​={0,1,2,…} when 000 is included.

  • Z={ …,−2,−1,0,1,2,… }\mathbb{Z}= \left\{\,\dots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\dots\,\right\}Z={…,−2,−1,0,1,2,…} — the integers; closed under subtraction.

  • Q={ pq  :  p∈Z, q∈N }\mathbb{Q}= \left\{\,\dfrac{p}{q} \;:\; p\in\mathbb{Z},\ q\in\mathbb{N}\,\right\}Q={qp​:p∈Z, q∈N} — the rationals; closed under division by nonzero rationals.

  • R\mathbb{R}R — the real numbers; informally the “number line with no gaps” (this completeness is made precise in Analysis). It contains Q\mathbb{Q}Q together with numbers such as 2\sqrt22​, π\piπ and e\mathrm{e}e that are not fractions.

  • C={ a+b i  :  a,b∈R }\mathbb{C}= \left\{\,a+b\,\mathrm{i} \;:\; a,b\in\mathbb{R}\,\right\}C={a+bi:a,b∈R}, where i\mathrm{i}i is a symbol with i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1 — the complex numbers, the subject of Units 2–3.

These sit inside one another, N⊂Z⊂Q⊂R⊂C\mathbb{N}\subset \mathbb{Z}\subset \mathbb{Q}\subset \mathbb{R}\subset \mathbb{C}N⊂Z⊂Q⊂R⊂C.

Each extension removes an obstruction. Subtraction can leave N\mathbb{N}N (e.g. 3−53-53−5), so we pass to Z\mathbb{Z}Z; division can leave Z\mathbb{Z}Z (e.g. 1÷21\div 21÷2), so we pass to Q\mathbb{Q}Q; taking limits or roots can leave Q\mathbb{Q}Q (e.g. 2\sqrt 22​), so we pass to R\mathbb{R}R; and the equation x2+1=0x^2+1=0x2+1=0 has no real solution, which forces the step to C\mathbb{C}C.

Rational and irrational numbers

A real number is rational if it can be written as a fraction of integers and irrational otherwise. A second, independent classification cuts across this one.

A real (or complex) number is algebraic if it is a root of some nonzero polynomial with integer coefficients, and transcendental otherwise.

Every number is algebraic or transcendental. Every rational is algebraic (p/qp/qp/q is a root of qx−pqx-pqx−p), and so are some irrationals: 2\sqrt22​ is a root of x2−2x^2-2x2−2, and 53\sqrt[3]{5}35​ a root of x3−5x^3-5x3−5. The transcendentals, such as π\piπ and e\mathrm{e}e, are the numbers that are roots of no integer polynomial; they are necessarily irrational, though they can still be approximated by rationals such as π≈227\pi\approx\tfrac{22}{7}π≈722​. The next example shows the standard way to prove a number irrational.

Suppose, for contradiction, that 2=pq\sqrt2=\tfrac pq2​=qp​ with p,q∈Np,q\in\mathbb{N}p,q∈N sharing no common factor. Squaring gives p2=2q2p^2=2q^2p2=2q2, so p2p^2p2 is even, hence ppp is even, say p=2kp=2kp=2k. Then 4k2=2q24k^2=2q^24k2=2q2, i.e. q2=2k2q^2=2k^2q2=2k2, so q2q^2q2 is even and qqq is even too. But then ppp and qqq share the factor 222, contradicting our assumption. Therefore no such fraction exists and 2\sqrt22​ is irrational.

Inequalities

Inequalities obey rules close to those for equations, with one crucial exception.

For real numbers one may add or subtract the same quantity on both sides freely, and multiply or divide both sides by a positive number. Multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign. Also, for a>0a>0a>0, ∣x∣≤a  ⟺  −a≤x≤a,∣x∣≥a  ⟺  x≤−a or x≥a.\left|x\right|\le a \;\Longleftrightarrow\;-a\le x\le a, \qquad \left|x\right|\ge a \;\Longleftrightarrow\;x\le -a \ \text{or}\ x\ge a .∣x∣≤a⟺−a≤x≤a,∣x∣≥a⟺x≤−a or x≥a.

Six questions on the ideas this unit is most often misunderstood on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

Which is the correct interval notation for {x∈R:x≥3}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R} : x\ge 3\right\}{x∈R:x≥3}?

The two ends are decided by different considerations. At the left, 333 is a genuine real number and the condition x≥3x\ge 3x≥3 admits it, so that end is closed and takes a square bracket — which rules out (3,∞)(3,\infty)(3,∞). At the right, ∞\infty∞ is not a real number at all; it is shorthand for 'grows without bound', so it can never be an element of the set. A boundary at ∞\infty∞ therefore always takes a round bracket, which rules out [3,∞][3,\infty][3,∞].

To solve 2x+1x−3≥0\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\ge 0x−32x+1​≥0, a student multiplies both sides by x−3x-3x−3. What is wrong with that step?

You may add or subtract anything, and you may multiply or divide by a positive number, but multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign. Here x−3x-3x−3 is negative for x<3x<3x<3 and positive for x>3x>3x>3, so no single step can serve both cases, and the student's answer will be wrong on half the line. The safe route is a sign table on the intervals cut out by the zero x=−12x=-\tfrac12x=−21​ and the pole x=3x=3x=3. (Note x=3x=3x=3 is not a lost solution — it is excluded, because the expression is undefined there.)

As a map R→R\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}R→R, f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2f(x)=x2 is neither injective nor surjective. What happens to the same rule as a map [0,∞)→[0,∞)[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)[0,∞)→[0,∞)?

A function is its rule together with its domain and codomain, and changing those can change the verdict. Squaring is strictly increasing on [0,∞)[0,\infty)[0,∞), so no two inputs share an output and it is injective — the pair f(−1)=f(1)f(-1)=f(1)f(−1)=f(1) that broke injectivity is no longer available, because −1-1−1 has left the domain. Its outputs fill [0,∞)[0,\infty)[0,∞) exactly, which is the stated codomain, so it is also surjective. Being both, it is bijective, and the inverse is the non-negative square root.

With f(x)=xf(x)=\sqrt{x}f(x)=x​ and g(x)=1−x2g(x)=1-x^2g(x)=1−x2, the composite (g∘f)(x)(g\circ f)(x)(g∘f)(x) simplifies to 1−x1-x1−x. What is its domain?

How is the graph of y=f(x−2)y=f(x-2)y=f(x−2) related to the graph of y=f(x)y=f(x)y=f(x)?

Changes inside the bracket act on the input, and therefore act backwards from how they read. To get the same output the new function needed, x−2x-2x−2 must equal the old input: if the old graph had a feature at x=ax=ax=a, the new one has it at x=a+2x=a+2x=a+2, which is to the right. Given arctan⁡1=π4\arctan 1=\tfrac{\pi}{4}arctan1=4π​, how many solutions does tan⁡x=1\tan x=1tanx=1 have in [0,2π)[0,2\pi)[0,2π)?

An inverse trigonometric function returns a single value, taken from the one branch on which the original function was inverted; the equation itself has as many solutions as the periodicity allows, and reading only the principal value off a calculator is the standard way to lose them. Since tan⁡\tantan has period π\piπ — not 2π2\pi2π — the second solution is π4+π=5π4\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\pi=\tfrac{5\pi}{4}4π​+π=45π​, and the next one, 9π4\tfrac{9\pi}{4}49π​, falls outside the stated range. Over all of R\mathbb{R}R there really are infinitely many, but the question fixes a window.
