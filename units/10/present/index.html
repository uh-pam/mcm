Unit 10

Integration II: techniques and applications

By the end of this unit you will be able to

  • Integrate by parts, by partial fractions, and by trigonometric methods.
  • Compute areas between curves and volumes of revolution.
  • Find the average value of a function.
  • Evaluate improper integrals and find probabilities and expectations from a density.
  • Set up definite integrals from a geometric or physical description.

This unit completes Unit 9’s toolkit with integration by parts, partial fractions and the trigonometric methods, then puts it to work on areas between curves, volumes of revolution, average values and the expectation of a probability distribution, which leads to integrals that run to infinity.

Where this fits. By parts reverses the product rule of Unit 7, as substitution reversed the chain rule; partial fractions integrate the rational functions of Unit 8; the trigonometric methods use the identities of Units 1 and 3 and Unit 9’s table. The applications build on Unit 9’s area reading and its Gaussian. This is the last unit of the module.

Further techniques

Integration by parts

Substitution reverses the chain rule. The remaining workhorse, integration by parts, reverses the product rule. Starting from (uv)′=u′v+uv′(uv)'=u'v+uv'(uv)′=u′v+uv′ of Unit 7 and integrating both sides, uv=∫u′v  dx+∫uv′  dxuv=\int u'v\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\int uv'\,\,\mathrm{d}xuv=∫u′vdx+∫uv′dx; rearranging isolates one of the two integrals in terms of the other.

A reliable order of preference for the part to call uuu is the mnemonic LIATE.

Partial fractions

A rational function—a ratio of polynomials—can be split into a sum of simpler fractions, each of which is on the standard-integral table or yields a logarithm or an arctangent. This is the reverse of putting fractions over a common denominator.

  1. If the degree of PPP is at least that of QQQ (an improper fraction), first divide to get a polynomial plus a proper remainder.

Setting up integrals from a description

The hardest part of an applied integral is rarely the integration; it is the setting up—turning a geometric or physical description into ∫ab(⋯ )  dx\int_a^b(\cdots)\,\,\mathrm{d}x∫ab​(⋯)dx. Every application in Section 2 follows one pattern, worth stating on its own.

  1. Slice the quantity into thin pieces indexed by a variable (here xxx), each of “width”  dx\,\mathrm{d}xdx.

  2. Write the contribution of one typical slice as (something)× dx(\text{something})\times\,\mathrm{d}x(something)×dx, where the “something” is evaluated at xxx—a height, an area, a density, a force.

  3. Add the slices by integrating over the range the variable covers.

Recognising that shared structure—one quantity assembled by summing infinitely many infinitesimal contributions—is the real content of integral calculus, and the reason it reaches so far beyond the area problem that introduced it in Unit 9.

Looking back. This completes the module. We began with the language of functions and the number systems they inhabit, crossed into the complex plane, then built the linear algebra of vectors, matrices and eigensystems, and closed with the differential and integral calculus of one variable. Each strand has a sequel: the linear algebra continues into a full course where the eigenvalues return as the spectral theorem and the singular value decomposition; the calculus extends to several variables, series and differential equations in the dedicated Calculus module; and for physicists the same tools reappear as normal modes, work integrals and equations of motion. The toolkit assembled across these ten units is the shared foundation beneath all of it.
