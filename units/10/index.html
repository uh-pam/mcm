Mathematical and Computational Methods
Lecture 10: Integration II: techniques and applications

Lecture 9 built the definite integral, the Fundamental Theorem, and the workhorse method of substitution. This lecture completes the toolkit and then puts it to use. We add the three techniques that reach the integrands substitution alone cannot—integration by parts (the product rule reversed), partial fractions (for rational functions), and the trigonometric methods (identities, and trigonometric substitution)—and then turn integration loose on the problems it was made for: areas between curves, volumes of revolution, average values, and the expectation of a probability distribution. The last of these carries us, finally, to integrals that run to infinity.

Where this fits. Each technique mirrors a rule of differentiation: by parts reverses the product rule of Lecture 7, just as substitution reversed the chain rule. Partial fractions integrate the rational functions whose graphs you sketched in Lecture 8, and the trigonometric methods lean on the identities of Lectures 1 and 3 and the inverse-trigonometric integrals tabulated in Lecture 9. The applications build directly on the area reading of the definite integral (Lecture 9) and the curve analysis of Lecture 8; the probability strand ties together the data-science thread of the course (the sigmoid of Lecture 1, gradient descent in Lecture 8, the Gaussian of Lecture 9) and the physics one (decay, work). This is the last calculus lecture, and the last of the module; Taylor series and differential equations remain with the dedicated Calculus module.

Substitution reverses the chain rule. The remaining workhorse, integration by parts, reverses the product rule. Applications of the definite integral

The definite integral was built as an area; we now use it for areas, for the volumes that areas sweep out, for averages, and—reading an area as a probability—for the expected value of a random quantity.

The average value of a function

The mean of finitely many numbers is their sum over their count. For a function on [a,b][a,b][a,b], the “sum” is an integral and the “count” is the length b−ab-ab−a.

The average value of a continuous fff over [a,b][a,b][a,b] is fˉ=1b−a∫abf(x)  dx.\bar f=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x .fˉ​=b−a1​∫ab​f(x)dx. Equivalently, fˉ\bar ffˉ​ is the height of the rectangle on [a,b][a,b][a,b] whose area equals the area under fff.

The average value of f(x)=4−x2f(x)=4-x^2f(x)=4−x2 over [0,2][0,2][0,2] is fˉ=12−0∫02(4−x2)  dx=12[4x−13x3]02=12(8−83)=83.\bar f=\frac{1}{2-0}\int_0^2\bigl(4-x^2\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}x =\frac12\Bigl[4x-\tfrac13 x^3\Bigr]_0^2=\frac12\Bigl(8-\tfrac{8}{3}\Bigr)=\frac{8}{3}.fˉ​=2−01​∫02​(4−x2)dx=21​[4x−31​x3]02​=21​(8−38​)=38​.

The equal-area rectangle’s top is drawn meeting the graph, but does fff actually attain the height fˉ\bar ffˉ​ somewhere? For a continuous function it does, and this is worth recording.

If fff is continuous on [a,b][a,b][a,b], there is a point c∈[a,b]c\in[a,b]c∈[a,b] with f(c)=fˉf(c)=\bar ff(c)=fˉ​; that is, ∫abf(x)  dx=f(c) (b−a).\int_a^b f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x=f(c)\,(b-a) .∫ab​f(x)dx=f(c)(b−a).

Proof. Being continuous on a closed interval, fff attains a minimum mmm and a maximum MMM there (Lecture 8). Since m≤f(x)≤Mm\le f(x)\le Mm≤f(x)≤M on all of [a,b][a,b][a,b], integrating preserves the inequalities, m(b−a)≤∫abf≤M(b−a)m(b-a)\le\int_a^b f\le M(b-a)m(b−a)≤∫ab​f≤M(b−a), and dividing by b−a>0b-a>0b−a>0 gives m≤fˉ≤Mm\le\bar f\le Mm≤fˉ​≤M. The intermediate value theorem (Lecture 8) says a continuous fff takes every value between its minimum and maximum; in particular it takes the value fˉ\bar ffˉ​ at some c∈[a,b]c\in[a,b]c∈[a,b]. ◻

This is precisely why the equal-area rectangle’s top really does cross the graph: continuity forces the average to be a genuine height of fff, not merely a number between its extremes.

The average value weights every point of [a,b][a,b][a,b] equally. If instead some outcomes are more likely than others, we weight by a density—and the average becomes an expectation. To express it for quantities with no upper bound, we first need integrals over an infinite range.

An integral over an unbounded interval is defined as a limit of ordinary integrals, ∫a∞f(x)  dx=lim⁡t→∞∫atf(x)  dx,∫−∞bf(x)  dx=lim⁡s→−∞∫sbf(x)  dx.\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^{t} f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x , \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{b} f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{s\to-\infty}\int_{s}^{b} f(x)\,\,\mathrm{d}x .∫a∞​f(x)dx=t→∞lim​∫at​f(x)dx,∫−∞b​f(x)dx=s→−∞lim​∫sb​f(x)dx. Setting up integrals from a description

The hardest part of an applied integral is rarely the integration; it is the setting up—turning a geometric or physical description into ∫ab(⋯ )  dx\int_a^b(\cdots)\,\,\mathrm{d}x∫ab​(⋯)dx. Every example above follows one pattern, worth stating on its own.

  1. Slice the quantity into thin pieces indexed by a variable (here xxx), each of “width”  dx\,\mathrm{d}xdx.

  2. Write the contribution of one typical slice as (something)× dx(\text{something})\times\,\mathrm{d}x(something)×dx, where the “something” is evaluated at xxx—a height, an area, a density, a force.

  3. Add the slices by integrating over the range the variable covers.

A rod occupies 0≤x≤L0\le x\le L0≤x≤L with linear density ρ(x)=ρ0(1+xL)\rho(x)=\rho_0\bigl(1+\tfrac{x}{L}\bigr)ρ(x)=ρ0​(1+Lx​) (it is denser towards one end). The same three steps—slice, weight, integrate—produced the area, volume and probability integrals above. They work just as well when the “something” carried by each slice is itself a varying area, or a distance.

That centre of mass, ∫x  dm/ ⁣∫ dm\int x\,\,\mathrm{d}m\big/\!\int\,\mathrm{d}m∫xdm/∫dm, is itself a density-weighted average of position—the same construction as the expectation E[X]\mathbb{E}[X]E[X] of Section 2.

The recipe is indifferent to the discipline: a slice carries mass for the physicist, probability for the statistician, area or volume for the geometer. Recognising that shared structure—one quantity assembled by summing infinitely many infinitesimal contributions—is the real content of integral calculus, and the reason it reaches so far beyond the area problem that introduced it in Lecture 9.
