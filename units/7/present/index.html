Differentiation I: limits and the rules

By the end of this unit you will be able to

  • Evaluate one-sided and infinite limits and limits at infinity, and locate asymptotes.
  • Define continuity and explain why differentiability implies continuity.
  • Differentiate from first principles and read the derivative three ways.

This unit builds the central tool of applied mathematics, the derivative, from the ground up. The limit pins down continuity and then the derivative, which we read three ways: as the slope of a tangent, as a rate of change, and as a sensitivity. The standard derivatives and the rules (sum, product, quotient and chain) then differentiate almost any function by hand, and parametric, implicit and logarithmic differentiation handle functions not given as a tidy y=f(x)y=f(x)y=f(x).

Where this fits. The raw materials are from Unit 1: the elementary functions, their domains, monotonicity and composition (which becomes the chain rule). Unit 8 puts the derivative to work on maxima and minima, curve sketching and optimisation, and Units 9–10 reverse it as integration.

Continuity

The most convenient functions are those with no jumps or breaks, so that the limit at a point is simply the value there. This is continuity.

A function fff is continuous at a point aaa of its domain if lim⁡x→af(x)=f(a),\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) ,x→alim​f(x)=f(a), that is, the limit exists and equals the function’s value. fff is continuous on an interval if it is continuous at every point of that interval.

Three things must hold at once: f(a)f(a)f(a) is defined, the limit exists, and the two agree. Informally, a continuous function is one whose graph can be drawn without lifting the pen: a jump (like the step function above) breaks continuity, and at a vertical asymptote there is no finite limit, so the function is discontinuous there if it is defined there at all. At an endpoint of the domain only one side exists, so the limit is taken from that side: x\sqrt{x}x​ is continuous at 000 because x→0\sqrt{x}\to0x​→0 as x→0+x\to0^{+}x→0+. The functions we use constantly—polynomials, ex\mathrm{e}^{x}ex, ln⁡x\ln xlnx, sin⁡x\sin xsinx, cos⁡x\cos xcosx, and their sums, products, quotients and compositions—are all continuous on their domains, which is why their limits are found simply by substituting.

Three readings of the derivative

The single number f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) carries three complementary meanings, and fluency in calculus means hearing all three at once.

  • Geometric — a slope. f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) is the slope of the tangent to the curve y=f(x)y=f(x)y=f(x), measuring how steeply the graph rises or falls.

  • Physical — a rate of change. If a quantity depends on time, its derivative is the instantaneous rate at which it changes: the derivative of position is velocity, and differentiating once more gives acceleration.

  • Sensitivity — a response to a nudge. f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) measures how strongly the output responds to a small change in the input: for a small nudge δx\delta xδx, the definition gives f(x+δx)≈f(x)+f′(x) δxf(x+\delta x)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,\delta xf(x+δx)≈f(x)+f′(x)δx, so a large ∣f′(x)∣\left|f'(x)\right|∣f′(x)∣ means a tiny nudge in xxx produces a big change in fff. In data science this is decisive—if a model’s error LLL depends on a parameter www, then  dL dw\frac{\,\mathrm{d}L}{\,\mathrm{d}w}dwdL​ says which way, and how hard, to adjust www to reduce the error. Further techniques

The rules so far assume yyy is given explicitly as f(x)f(x)f(x). Three further techniques extend differentiation to functions described in other ways: through a parameter, implicitly by an equation, or as an awkward product whose logarithm is simpler.
