With the algebra and geometry of the module’s first half behind us, we change subject to the central tool of applied mathematics: the derivative. This unit builds it from the ground up. We start with the limit—the idea of a value approached but not necessarily reached—use it to pin down what it means for a function to be continuous, and then to define the derivative, which we learn to read three ways at once: geometrically as the slope of a tangent, physically as a rate of change, and—for the data-science students—as a sensitivity. We then assemble the standard derivatives and the rules (sum, product, quotient and chain) that let us differentiate almost any function by hand, before finishing with three techniques—parametric, implicit and logarithmic differentiation—for functions that are not handed to us as a tidy y=f(x)y=f(x)y=f(x).

Where this fits. The raw materials are all from Unit 1: the elementary functions, their domains, composition (which becomes the chain rule), and monotonicity. Differentiation is the engine of Unit 8, where we use it to find maxima and minima, sketch curves and solve optimisation problems, and it is the exact reverse of the integration developed in Units 9–10. The standard derivatives and rules collected here are a reference point you will return to for the rest of the course and beyond.

Continuity

The most convenient functions are those with no jumps or breaks, so that the limit at a point is simply the value there. This is continuity.

A function fff is continuous at a point aaa of its domain if lim⁡x→af(x)=f(a),\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a) ,x→alim​f(x)=f(a), that is, the limit exists and equals the function’s value. fff is continuous on an interval if it is continuous at every point of that interval.

Three things must hold at once: f(a)f(a)f(a) is defined, the limit exists, and the two agree. Informally, a continuous function is one whose graph can be drawn without lifting the pen: a jump (like the step function above) breaks continuity, while at a vertical asymptote the function is undefined, so continuity does not even apply there. The functions we use constantly—polynomials, ex\mathrm{e}^{x}ex, ln⁡x\ln xlnx, sin⁡x\sin xsinx, cos⁡x\cos xcosx, and their sums, products, quotients and compositions—are all continuous on their domains, which is why their limits are found simply by substituting.

Three readings of the derivative

The single number f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) carries three complementary meanings, and fluency in calculus means hearing all three at once.

  • Geometric — a slope. f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) is the slope of the tangent to the curve y=f(x)y=f(x)y=f(x), measuring how steeply the graph rises or falls.

  • Physical — a rate of change. If a quantity depends on time, its derivative is the instantaneous rate at which it changes: the derivative of position is velocity, and differentiating once more gives acceleration.

  • Sensitivity — a response to a nudge. f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) measures how strongly the output responds to a small change in the input: a large ∣f′(x)∣\left|f'(x)\right|∣f′(x)∣ means a tiny nudge in xxx produces a big change in fff. In data science this is decisive—if a model’s error LLL depends on a parameter www, then  dL dw\frac{\,\mathrm{d}L}{\,\mathrm{d}w}dwdL​ says which way, and how hard, to adjust www to reduce the error. That is precisely the information the gradient-descent method of Unit 8 exploits.

The chain rule

The most powerful rule differentiates a composition—a function of a function, such as sin⁡(x2)\sin(x^2)sin(x2) or e3x\mathrm{e}^{3x}e3x. Recall from Unit 1 that recognising the inner and outer functions is the key skill; the chain rule then multiplies their derivatives.

If y=f(u)y=f(u)y=f(u) and u=g(x)u=g(x)u=g(x), so that y=f(g(x))y=f\bigl(g(x)\bigr)y=f(g(x)), then  dy dx= dy du⋅ du dx=f′(g(x)) g′(x).\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}u}\cdot\frac{\,\mathrm{d}u}{\,\mathrm{d}x}=f'\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x).dxdy​=dudy​⋅dxdu​=f′(g(x))g′(x).

In Leibniz notation the rule reads as though the  du\,\mathrm{d}udu’s cancel—a useful mnemonic, though the  du\,\mathrm{d}udu are not literally numbers. In words: differentiate the outer function (keeping the inner one intact), then multiply by the derivative of the inner function.

For y=sin⁡(x2)y=\sin(x^2)y=sin(x2), take the outer f(u)=sin⁡uf(u)=\sin uf(u)=sinu and inner u=x2u=x^2u=x2. Then f′(u)=cos⁡uf'(u)=\cos uf′(u)=cosu and u′=2xu'=2xu′=2x, so  dy dx=cos⁡(x2)⋅2x=2xcos⁡(x2).\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}=\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x=2x\cos(x^2).dxdy​=cos(x2)⋅2x=2xcos(x2).

A frequent special case is a function raised to a power, y=(g(x))ny=\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^ny=(g(x))n, for which the chain rule gives y′=n(g(x))n−1g′(x)y'=n\bigl(g(x)\bigr)^{n-1}g'(x)y′=n(g(x))n−1g′(x).

Use the quotient rule with u=(2x+1)3u=(2x+1)^3u=(2x+1)3 and v=(x2+1)1/2v=(x^2+1)^{1/2}v=(x2+1)1/2, each of which needs the chain rule: u′=3(2x+1)2⋅2=6(2x+1)2,v′=12(x2+1)−1/2⋅2x=xx2+1.u'=3(2x+1)^2\cdot 2=6(2x+1)^2, \qquad v'=\tfrac12(x^2+1)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}.u′=3(2x+1)2⋅2=6(2x+1)2,v′=21​(x2+1)−1/2⋅2x=x2+1​x​. Then  dy dx=u′v−uv′v2=6(2x+1)2x2+1−(2x+1)3xx2+1x2+1.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2} =\frac{6(2x+1)^2\sqrt{x^2+1}-(2x+1)^3\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}}{x^2+1}.dxdy​=v2u′v−uv′​=x2+16(2x+1)2x2+1​−(2x+1)3x2+1​x​​. The chain and quotient rules give the derivative of the logistic function σ(x)=(1+e−x)−1\sigma(x)=\bigl(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}\bigr)^{-1}σ(x)=(1+e−x)−1 from Unit 1. Differentiating, σ′(x)=−(1+e−x)−2⋅(−e−x)=e−x(1+e−x)2=σ(x)(1−σ(x)),\sigma'(x)=-\bigl(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}\bigr)^{-2}\cdot(-\mathrm{e}^{-x}) =\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{-x})^2}=\sigma(x)\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr),σ′(x)=−(1+e−x)−2⋅(−e−x)=(1+e−x)2e−x​=σ(x)(1−σ(x)), the last step using 1−σ(x)=e−x1+e−x1-\sigma(x)=\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-x}}1−σ(x)=1+e−xe−x​. This tidy form—the derivative expressed in terms of the function itself—is what makes the sigmoid so convenient for training models, as the data-science students will see again in Unit 8.

Further techniques

The rules so far assume yyy is given explicitly as f(x)f(x)f(x). Three further techniques extend differentiation to functions described in other ways: through a parameter, implicitly by an equation, or as an awkward product whose logarithm is simpler.

Parametric differentiation

Sometimes a curve is traced out by giving both coordinates in terms of a third variable, the parameter: x=x(t),y=y(t).x=x(t), \qquad y=y(t).x=x(t),y=y(t). The circle x=rcos⁡t, y=rsin⁡tx=r\cos t,\ y=r\sin tx=rcost, y=rsint and the ellipse x=acos⁡t, y=bsin⁡tx=a\cos t,\ y=b\sin tx=acost, y=bsint are typical; so is the position of a particle, with ttt the time. To find the slope  dy dx\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}dxdy​ without eliminating ttt, apply the chain rule.

If xxx and yyy are differentiable functions of a parameter ttt and  dx dt≠0\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}x}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\ne0dtdx​=0, then  dy dx= dy/ dt dx/ dt, d2y dx2= d dt ⁣( dy dx) dx/ dt.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y/\,\mathrm{d}t}{\,\mathrm{d}x/\,\mathrm{d}t}, \qquad \frac{\,\mathrm{d}^{2} y}{\,\mathrm{d}x^{2}}=\frac{\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\!\left(\dfrac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}\right)}{\,\mathrm{d}x/\,\mathrm{d}t}.dxdy​=dx/dtdy/dt​,dx2d2y​=dx/dtdtd​(dxdy​)​.

Implicit differentiation

Many curves are defined by an equation relating xxx and yyy that cannot be solved neatly for yyy, such as x2+y2=25x^2+y^2=25x2+y2=25 or sin⁡(x+y)=3xy\sin(x+y)=3xysin(x+y)=3xy. We can still differentiate the equation as it stands, treating yyy as an unknown function of xxx and applying the chain rule to every term involving yyy. The key move is  d dx[ g(y) ]=g′(y)  dy dx,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}x}\bigl[\,g(y)\,\bigr]=g'(y)\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x},dxd​[g(y)]=g′(y)dxdy​, so for instance  d dx(y2)=2y  dy dx\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}x}(y^2)=2y\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}dxd​(y2)=2ydxdy​ and, by the product rule,  d dx(xy)=y+x  dy dx\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}x}(xy)=y+x\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}dxd​(xy)=y+xdxdy​. Logarithmic differentiation

When a function is a tall product or quotient of factors, or has the variable in both base and exponent (as in y=xxy=x^xy=xx), taking the natural logarithm first turns products into sums and exponents into multipliers, which are far easier to differentiate. The tool is the chain-rule fact  d dxln⁡∣f(x)∣=f′(x)f(x),\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}x}\ln\bigl|f(x)\bigr|=\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)},dxd​ln​f(x)​=f(x)f′(x)​, the logarithmic derivative of fff. Taking ln⁡∣f∣\ln\left|f\right|ln∣f∣ rather than ln⁡f\ln flnf matters: it makes the step legitimate wherever the factors are nonzero, whatever their sign, and the absolute values then cancel in the final answer. The recipe is: take ln⁡∣ ⋅ ∣\ln\left|\,\cdot\,\right|ln∣⋅∣ of both sides, simplify with the log laws, differentiate implicitly, and multiply back by yyy.

The power rule does not apply (xxx is in the base) and neither does the exponential rule (xxx is in the exponent). Take logs: ln⁡y=xln⁡x\ln y=x\ln xlny=xlnx. Differentiating the right-hand side by the product rule, and the left by the chain rule, 1y dy dx=ln⁡x+x⋅1x=ln⁡x+1,\frac{1}{y}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}=\ln x+x\cdot\frac1x=\ln x+1,y1​dxdy​=lnx+x⋅x1​=lnx+1, so  dy dx=y (ln⁡x+1)=xx(ln⁡x+1)\frac{\,\mathrm{d}y}{\,\mathrm{d}x}=y\,(\ln x+1)=x^x(\ln x+1)dxdy​=y(lnx+1)=xx(lnx+1).
