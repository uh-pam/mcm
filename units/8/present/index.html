The intermediate value theorem locates a root—it tells us a continuous, sign-changing fff has a zero in (1,2)(1,2)(1,2)—but it does not say where. Newton’s method is fast but not foolproof. Started near a simple root (one where f′≠0f'\ne0f′=0) it converges rapidly, but a poor starting guess can send it off to a different root or away altogether, and if f′(xn)f'(x_n)f′(xn​) is near zero the step f(xn)/f′(xn)f(x_n)/f'(x_n)f(xn​)/f′(xn​) becomes huge and the iteration can jump wildly. The slow-but-safe complement is bisection, built on the intermediate value theorem: given a sign change on an interval, test the midpoint, keep whichever half still has a sign change, and repeat. Each step halves an interval that is guaranteed to contain a root, so the root can never be lost, where Newton trades that guarantee for speed. Optimisation

Solving optimisation problems

Optimisation asks for the largest or smallest value of some quantity. The calculus is the easy part; the skill is translating a description into a function of one variable and identifying the interval on which it is to be optimised.

  1. Name the quantity QQQ to be optimised and the variables it depends on.

  2. Use the constraints to write QQQ as a function of a single variable, and state the domain (the physically meaningful interval).

  3. Find the critical points: solve Q′=0Q'=0Q′=0, and add any interior point of the domain where Q′Q'Q′ fails to exist (a corner or a cusp—a sharp point of the graph—is a candidate too: Q(x)=(x3)2Q(x)=\bigl(\sqrt[3]{x}\bigr)^{2}Q(x)=(3x​)2 has its minimum at just such a point).

  4. Identify the optimum among the critical points and, on a closed interval, the endpoints; classify with the second-derivative test where convenient.

The endpoint check in step 4 is not optional: by the extreme value theorem a continuous function on a closed interval attains its extremes somewhere, but that somewhere may be a boundary rather than a stationary point. Comparing the values at the critical points and the endpoints, the closed-interval method, is therefore guaranteed to find the answer. On [−3,3][-3,3][−3,3] the function x3−3xx^3-3xx3−3x of Example [ex:classify] has the values f(−3)=−18f(-3)=-18f(−3)=−18, f(−1)=2f(-1)=2f(−1)=2, f(1)=−2f(1)=-2f(1)=−2 and f(3)=18f(3)=18f(3)=18: its absolute extremes are at the endpoints, and its local maximum and minimum are neither. On an open domain there are no endpoints to compare; look instead at how QQQ behaves towards the ends of the domain.

Gradient descent

Solving f′(x)=0f'(x)=0f′(x)=0 by hand works for the textbook functions above, but in data science a quantity to be minimised—an error or loss LLL—may depend on thousands of parameters through a formula far too tangled to solve exactly. We instead minimise numerically, and the derivative shows the way.

Recall the third reading of the derivative from Unit 7: f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) is a sensitivity, the direction and rate in which fff responds to a nudge in xxx. With many parameters the single derivative becomes a vector of derivatives (the gradient), and the step is taken against it; the principle is unchanged.
