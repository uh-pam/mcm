Unit 7 built the derivative and the rules for computing it. This unit puts that machinery to work. We begin with the geometry it controls—the tangent and normal lines—and with four theorems that underpin everything which follows: the intermediate-value, extreme-value and mean-value theorems, and the rule for differentiating an inverse, together with l’Hôpital’s rule for awkward limits. We then use the first and second derivatives to locate and classify the turning points of a curve and to sketch it accurately, and finally to solve optimisation problems—closing with gradient descent, the numerical idea that turns the derivative into an engine for minimisation across the sciences and data science.

Where this fits. Everything rests on Unit 7: the derivative and its three readings, the rules, higher derivatives, and the continuity it requires. The mean-value theorem is what licenses the increasing/decreasing test of Section 2, and the extreme-value theorem is what licenses the endpoint check in the optimisation of Section 3. The curve-sketching skills feed directly into the area and volume problems of Units 9–10, and the optimisation and gradient-descent material is foundational for the data-science and physics students alike.

The Mean Value Theorem

Where the previous two theorems used only continuity, the mean value theorem uses differentiability, and it is the workhorse behind the curve-analysis of Section 2.

Geometrically: somewhere strictly between aaa and bbb the tangent is parallel to the secant joining the endpoints—the instantaneous rate of change equals the average rate of change at least once.

The single most useful consequence concerns the sign of the derivative. If f′f'f′ is positive throughout an interval, then for any two points the right-hand side above is positive, so fff increases; and likewise for the other signs.

Newton’s method

The intermediate value theorem locates a root—it tells us a continuous, sign-changing fff has a zero in (1,2)(1,2)(1,2)—but it does not say where. To pin the root down we put the tangent line to work. Critical points and curve sketching

Critical points and the first-derivative test

A high or low point of a graph has a horizontal tangent, so the search for the peaks and troughs of a function starts by setting its derivative to zero.

Optimisation

Solving optimisation problems

Optimisation asks for the largest or smallest value of some quantity. The calculus is the easy part; the skill is translating a description into a function of one variable and identifying the interval on which it is to be optimised.

  1. Name the quantity QQQ to be optimised and the variables it depends on.

  2. Use the constraints to write QQQ as a function of a single variable, and state the domain (the physically meaningful interval).

  3. Find the critical points: solve Q′=0Q'=0Q′=0, and add any interior point of the domain where Q′Q'Q′ fails to exist (a corner or cusp is a candidate too—Q(x)=x2/3Q(x)=x^{2/3}Q(x)=x2/3 has its minimum at just such a point).

  4. Identify the optimum among the critical points and, on a closed interval, the endpoints; classify with the second-derivative test where convenient.

The endpoint check in step 4 is not optional: by the extreme value theorem a continuous function on a closed interval attains its extremes somewhere, but that somewhere may be a boundary rather than a stationary point. Checking the critical points and the endpoints is therefore guaranteed to find the answer.

Gradient descent

Solving f′(x)=0f'(x)=0f′(x)=0 by hand works for the textbook functions above, but in data science a quantity to be minimised—an error or loss LLL—may depend on thousands of parameters through a formula far too tangled to solve exactly. We instead minimise numerically, and the derivative shows the way.

Recall the third reading of the derivative from Unit 7: f′(x)f'(x)f′(x) is a sensitivity, the direction and rate in which fff responds to a nudge in xxx. Why does the extreme value theorem insist on a closed, bounded interval?

On (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) the values of f(x)=xf(x)=xf(x)=x climb arbitrarily close to 111 without ever reaching it, so there is no largest value; and on (0,1](0,1](0,1] the function 1/x1/x1/x is unbounded. Closedness and boundedness together rule out both failures. This matters practically rather than pedantically: it is what guarantees an optimisation problem has a best answer at all, and that checking the critical points together with the two endpoints exhausts every possibility. Endpoints need no derivative — they are handled by evaluation.

Newton's method converges far faster than trapping a root with the intermediate value theorem. What does it give up in exchange?

Once a sign change has been found, the intermediate value theorem has a root trapped and no later step can lose it — slow but certain. Newton instead follows the tangent, roughly doubling the number of correct digits each step, but nothing confines it to the bracket: a bad start can send it towards a different root or away entirely, and a near-zero derivative makes the correction f(xn)/f′(xn)f(x_n)/f'(x_n)f(xn​)/f′(xn​) enormous. It needs no learning rate — that is gradient descent — and it applies to any differentiable function, not only polynomials.
