With this unit the module changes subject. The first three units built the foundations—numbers, functions and the complex plane; the next three open the linear-algebra strand, whose objects are not single numbers but vectors and the arrays—matrices—that act on them, with the calculus following in the second half of the course. A vector carries both a size and a direction, as many quantities in geometry, mechanics and data do: a displacement, a velocity, a force, or a list of measurements. This unit introduces vectors in the plane and in space, their algebra (addition, scaling, magnitude), the two ways of multiplying them—the scalar (dot) and vector (cross) products—and the equations of lines and planes that they let us write down.

Where this fits. The coordinate plane of Unit 1 and school geometry—Pythagoras and the cosine rule—are the only prerequisites; no calculus is needed, so the linear-algebra units stand on their own. The dot product defined here generalises in one line to Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn and supplies the cosine similarity the data-science thread has been pointing towards. Two ideas are left at the level of intuition and made computational in Unit 5: testing vectors for independence, and—hiding inside the cross product—the determinant, used here only as a memory device and defined properly next time. The lines and planes of Section 3 are solution sets of linear systems, so their intersections return as the geometry of Unit 5, and the strand closes with eigensystems in Unit 6.

Vectors and their algebra

Scalars and vectors

A scalar is a quantity described by a single number—a temperature, a mass, a length. A vector needs a number and a direction: a wind of 303030 km/h towards the north-east, or a force of 101010 N downward. We draw a vector as an arrow whose length is its magnitude and whose heading is its direction.

Fix coordinate axes. A vector is an ordered list of real numbers, its components, v=(v1,v2)∈R2,v=(v1,v2,v3)∈R3,orv=(v1,…,vn)∈Rn.\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2)\in\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)\in\mathbb{R}^3, \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbf{v}=(v_1,\dots,v_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n .v=(v1​,v2​)∈R2,v=(v1​,v2​,v3​)∈R3,orv=(v1​,…,vn​)∈Rn. Vectors with the same number of components (the same dimension) are added, and a vector is scaled by a real number kkk, componentwise: u+v=(u1+v1,…,un+vn),kv=(kv1,…,kvn).\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=(u_1+v_1,\dots,u_n+v_n), \qquad k\mathbf{v}=(kv_1,\dots,kv_n).u+v=(u1​+v1​,…,un​+vn​),kv=(kv1​,…,kvn​). What makes Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn a space of vectors is exactly that it is closed under these two operations: adding two vectors, or scaling one, again gives a vector of the same kind. The zero vector 0=(0,…,0)\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)0=(0,…,0) is the identity for addition.

We write vectors in bold, u,v,w\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}u,v,w, and scalars in ordinary italic, a,k,ta, k, ta,k,t. (By hand, an underline v‾\underline{v}v​ or an arrow v→\overrightarrow{v}v is usual.)

In R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 and R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 a vector is pictured as a directed segment (an arrow) from an initial point to a terminal point. Two arrows with the same length and direction—one a parallel translate of the other—represent the same vector; sliding v\mathbf{v}v so its tail is at the origin, the coordinates of its tip are the components above.

It is often helpful to think of a nonzero vector as carrying a magnitude and a direction; but that picture is only a label for the data above, and it is the two operations—addition and scaling—not the words “magnitude” and “direction”—that make something a vector. (The abstract notion of a vector space, of which Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn is the first example, is developed in the Linear Algebra module.)

A vector’s components are read off the picture exactly as above: slide it so its initial point is the origin, and the coordinates of its terminal point are the components. Conversely, any two points determine a vector.

Magnitude and unit vectors

The length of a vector is read straight off its components by Pythagoras (Figure 2).

The magnitude (or norm, or length) of v=(v1,v2,v3)\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)v=(v1​,v2​,v3​) is ∥v∥=v12+v22+v32 ,\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2}\,,∥v∥=v12​+v22​+v32​​, with the obvious two-term version in R2\mathbb{R}^2R2. The distance between points PPP and QQQ is ∥PQ→∥\left\|\overrightarrow{PQ}\right\|​PQ​​.

A vector of length one is a unit vector; it records a pure direction. Dividing any nonzero vector by its own length produces the unit vector pointing the same way, a step called normalising.

Beyond three dimensions: Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn and a data-science note

Nothing in the component definitions used the number three. A list of nnn real numbers v=(v1,v2,…,vn)∈Rn,∥v∥=v12+⋯+vn2,\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n, \qquad \left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2},v=(v1​,v2​,…,vn​)∈Rn,∥v∥=v12​+⋯+vn2​​, is a vector in nnn-space, added, scaled and measured by the same formulas as before. We cannot draw Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn once nnn exceeds three, but the algebra is unchanged, and that is exactly what data science needs.

A record with nnn measured features—the pixels of an image, the word counts of a document, the readings of a sensor array—is a point of Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn, hence a vector. Their directions often matter more than their lengths (a document and its doubled copy should count as identical in subject), which is why the angle between two such vectors, introduced through the dot product in Section 2, becomes the standard cosine-similarity measure. The geometry of this unit is therefore not confined to two and three dimensions.

Linear combinations, independence and basis (intuition)

Adding scaled vectors, au+bv+cwa\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}+c\mathbf{w}au+bv+cw, gives a linear combination of them. Two questions recur throughout linear algebra: is a vector redundant—a combination of the others—and can a given set reach every vector?

A set of vectors is linearly independent if none of them is a linear combination of the rest—there is no redundancy. (Equivalently, and in the form you will meet again in Linear Algebra: the only linear combination that equals 0\mathbf{0}0 is the one with all coefficients zero.) A basis is an independent set large enough to build every vector in the space, each in exactly one way. In R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 the standard i,j,k\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}i,j,k form the basic example: two vectors are independent unless they are parallel, and three are independent unless they lie in a common plane. The systematic test for independence—and the procedure that finds the coefficients of a combination—is the solution of a linear system by Gaussian elimination, which we develop in Unit 5. For now the geometric picture suffices.

Products of vectors

There is no single “multiplication” of vectors. Two genuinely different products are useful: one returns a scalar and measures alignment; the other (in three dimensions) returns a vector perpendicular to its factors.

The scalar (dot) product

The dot product of u\mathbf{u}u and v\mathbf{v}v is the scalar u⋅v=u1v1+u2v2+u3v3(and u1v1+⋯+unvn in Rn).\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3 \qquad(\text{and } u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n \text{ in } \mathbb{R}^n).u⋅v=u1​v1​+u2​v2​+u3​v3​(and u1​v1​+⋯+un​vn​ in Rn).

The angle between two vectors

Solving the geometric form for the cosine gives the formula that drives everything else.

Orthogonality and projection

Vectors u\mathbf{u}u and v\mathbf{v}v are orthogonal (perpendicular) precisely when u⋅v=0.\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0 .u⋅v=0. The zero vector is taken to be orthogonal to everything.

The dot product also resolves one vector along another. The vector (cross) product

In three dimensions there is a second product, returning a vector perpendicular to both factors. It exists only in R3\mathbb{R}^3R3.

For u=(u1,u2,u3)\mathbf{u}=(u_1,u_2,u_3)u=(u1​,u2​,u3​) and v=(v1,v2,v3)\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)v=(v1​,v2​,v3​) in R3\mathbb{R}^3R3, u×v=(u2v3−u3v2,  u3v1−u1v3,  u1v2−u2v1).\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} =\bigl(u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\ \ u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\ \ u_1v_2-u_2v_1\bigr).u×v=(u2​v3​−u3​v2​,  u3​v1​−u1​v3​,  u1​v2​−u2​v1​).

The cross product is the natural language of rotation. The torque of a force F\mathbf{F}F applied at position r\mathbf{r}r from a pivot is τ=r×F\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}τ=r×F, and the magnetic part of the Lorentz force on a charge qqq moving with velocity v\mathbf{v}v in a field B\mathbf{B}B is q v×Bq\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}qv×B. Lines and planes

A point fixes where; a vector fixes which way. Together they pin down the two basic flat objects of space—the line and the plane—and the products of Section 2 turn those descriptions into equations.

Where a line meets a plane; the angle between two planes

With a line and a plane both in hand, two natural questions are pure dot-product geometry: where a line crosses a plane, and at what angle two planes meet. Each reuses the very objects built above.

A line in R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 or a plane in R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 is the solution set of a linear equation; asking where two planes meet, or whether three planes share a point, is asking to solve a system of them. Whether such a system has one solution, none, or infinitely many is the geometry of intersecting, parallel and coincident planes—and the algebra that decides it, Gaussian elimination, is the heart of the next unit.

Self-check

Six questions on the ideas this unit is most often misunderstood on. Answer before you look — a wrong answer you thought about is worth more than a right one you guessed.

For non-zero u\mathbf{u}u and v\mathbf{v}v, what does a negative value of u⋅v\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}u⋅v tell you?

Since cos⁡θ=u⋅v∥u∥ ∥v∥\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}cosθ=∥u∥∥v∥u⋅v​ and both lengths are positive, the sign of the dot product is exactly the sign of cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ: positive means acute, zero a right angle, negative obtuse. You do need the division to get the angle itself — the last option is right about that much — but not to get its type. Skipping the division altogether is a common slip, and it often produces a 'cosine' outside [−1,1][-1,1][−1,1], which is the cheapest error check available.

Is u×v=v×u\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{u}u×v=v×u for vectors in R3\mathbb{R}^3R3?

The cross product is anticommutative: v×u=−(u×v)\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{u}=-(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v})v×u=−(u×v). Both results have the same magnitude — the area of the same parallelogram — and both are perpendicular to the plane of u\mathbf{u}u and v\mathbf{v}v, so they are certainly parallel; they differ only in which of the two available directions they point, which the right-hand rule settles. The two agree only when u×v=0\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}u×v=0, which happens when the vectors are parallel — the opposite of the condition offered in the second option.

A line has direction d\mathbf{d}d and a plane has normal n\mathbf{n}n. What does d⋅n=0\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0d⋅n=0 tell you?

Substituting the line into the plane equation gives a linear equation in ttt whose coefficient is d⋅n\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}d⋅n. When that is non-zero there is exactly one root, hence one crossing point. When it vanishes the ttt terms disappear and what remains is either false — the line misses the plane entirely — or true for every ttt, in which case the whole line lies inside the plane. Note that d⋅n=0\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0d⋅n=0 makes the direction perpendicular to the normal, which is the opposite of the line being perpendicular to the plane; that case is d\mathbf{d}d parallel to n\mathbf{n}n.

The angle between two planes is computed from their normals. Why does one report ∣cos⁡θ∣|\cos\theta|∣cosθ∣ rather than cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ?

A plane has two opposite normals and nothing in the equation distinguishes them: writing −n-\mathbf{n}−n instead of n\mathbf{n}n flips the sign of the cosine and replaces the answer by its supplement. By convention the angle between two planes is the acute one, so taking the absolute value picks it out whichever normals happened to be written down. Cosines certainly can be negative, and unequal normal lengths are already dealt with by the division by ∥n1∥ ∥n2∥\|\mathbf{n}_1\|\,\|\mathbf{n}_2\|∥n1​∥∥n2​∥ in the formula.

A query is the vector q=(1,1,1)\mathbf{q}=(1,1,1)q=(1,1,1) and a document is d=(2,2,2)\mathbf{d}=(2,2,2)d=(2,2,2). What is their cosine similarity?

The calculation is 63⋅12=66=1\dfrac{6}{\sqrt3\cdot\sqrt{12}}=\dfrac{6}{6}=13​⋅12​6​=66​=1. Cosine similarity measures direction alone, so a document and a doubled copy of it count as identical in subject — exactly the behaviour wanted when comparing texts of different lengths. Since it is a cosine it can never exceed 111, which rules out the first two options before any arithmetic is done; that is the same range check that catches the error of forgetting to divide by the lengths.

You find that u⋅v=0\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0u⋅v=0. Does it follow that u=0\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}u=0 or v=0\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}v=0?

The simplest counterexample is (1,0)⋅(0,1)=0(1,0)\cdot(0,1)=0(1,0)⋅(0,1)=0, with neither factor zero. A dot product is not a product of numbers, and the zero-product rule of real arithmetic does not carry across: a vanishing dot product records perpendicularity, not degeneracy. That is precisely what makes it usable as an orthogonality test, and it holds in every dimension alike — in R2\mathbb{R}^2R2, in R3\mathbb{R}^3R3, and in the R4\mathbb{R}^4R4 word-count vectors of this unit.
