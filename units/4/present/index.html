Unit 4

Vectors

By the end of this unit you will be able to

  • Add, subtract and scale vectors, and find magnitudes and unit vectors.
  • Compute the dot product (angle, projection, orthogonality) and the cross product (normal, area).
  • Derive and use the equations of lines and planes.
  • Explain linear independence and basis, and interpret cosine similarity in Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn.

With this unit the module turns from numbers and functions to linear algebra, whose objects are vectors and the matrices that act on them. A vector carries a size and a direction, as a displacement, a velocity, a force or a list of measurements does. This unit develops their algebra, the two ways of multiplying them—the scalar (dot) and vector (cross) products—and the equations of the lines and planes they let us write down.

Where this fits. The coordinate plane of Unit 1, Pythagoras and the cosine rule are the only prerequisites; no calculus is needed. Two ideas stay at the level of intuition until Unit 5 makes them computational: testing vectors for independence, and the determinant hiding inside the cross product. The lines and planes of Section 3 are solution sets of linear systems, which Unit 5 solves, and the strand closes with eigensystems in Unit 6.

Vectors and their algebra

Scalars and vectors

A scalar is a quantity described by a single number—a temperature, a mass, a length. A vector needs a number and a direction: a wind of 303030 km/h towards the north-east, or a force of 101010 N downward. We draw a vector as an arrow whose length is its magnitude and whose heading is its direction.

Fix coordinate axes. A vector is an ordered list of real numbers, its components, v=(v1,v2)∈R2,v=(v1,v2,v3)∈R3,orv=(v1,…,vn)∈Rn.\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2)\in\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad\allowbreak \mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)\in\mathbb{R}^3, \qquad\allowbreak \text{or}\qquad\allowbreak \mathbf{v}=(v_1,\dots,v_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n .v=(v1​,v2​)∈R2,v=(v1​,v2​,v3​)∈R3,orv=(v1​,…,vn​)∈Rn. Vectors with the same number of components (the same dimension) are added, and a vector is scaled by a real number kkk, componentwise: u+v=(u1+v1,…,un+vn),kv=(kv1,…,kvn).\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=(u_1+v_1,\dots,u_n+v_n), \qquad\allowbreak k\mathbf{v}=(kv_1,\dots,kv_n).u+v=(u1​+v1​,…,un​+vn​),kv=(kv1​,…,kvn​). What makes Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn a space of vectors is exactly that it is closed under these two operations: adding two vectors, or scaling one, again gives a vector of the same kind. The zero vector 0=(0,…,0)\mathbf{0}=(0,\dots,0)0=(0,…,0) changes nothing when it is added: u+0=u\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{u}u+0=u.

We write vectors in bold, u,v,w\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}u,v,w, and scalars in ordinary italic, a,k,ta, k, ta,k,t. (By hand, an underline v‾\underline{v}v​ or an arrow v→\overrightarrow{v}v is usual.)

In R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 and R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 a vector is pictured as a directed segment (an arrow) from an initial point to a terminal point. Two arrows with the same length and direction—one a parallel translate of the other—represent the same vector; sliding v\mathbf{v}v so its tail is at the origin, the coordinates of its tip are the components above.

It is often helpful to think of a nonzero vector as carrying a magnitude and a direction; but that picture is only a label for the data above, and it is the two operations—addition and scaling—not the words “magnitude” and “direction”—that make something a vector. (The abstract notion of a vector space, of which Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn is the first example, is developed in the separate, later Linear Algebra module taken by the Mathematics and Data Science students.)

Magnitude and unit vectors

The length of a vector is read straight off its components by Pythagoras (Figure 2).

The magnitude (or norm, or length) of v=(v1,v2,v3)\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)v=(v1​,v2​,v3​) is ∥v∥=v12+v22+v32 ,\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2}\,,∥v∥=v12​+v22​+v32​​, with the obvious two-term version in R2\mathbb{R}^2R2. The distance between points PPP and QQQ is ∥PQ→∥\left\|\overrightarrow{PQ}\right\|​PQ​​.

A vector of length one is a unit vector; it records a pure direction. Dividing any nonzero vector by its own length produces the unit vector pointing the same way, a step called normalising.

For v=(2,−1,2)\mathbf{v}=(2,-1,2)v=(2,−1,2) the length is ∥v∥=4+1+4=3\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=\sqrt{4+1+4}=3∥v∥=4+1+4​=3, so the unit vector in its direction is v^=(23,−13,23)\hat{\mathbf{v}}=\bigl(\tfrac23,-\tfrac13,\tfrac23\bigr)v^=(32​,−31​,32​). Beyond three dimensions: Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn and a data-science note

Nothing in the component definitions used the number three. A list of nnn real numbers v=(v1,v2,…,vn)∈Rn,∥v∥=v12+⋯+vn2,\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n, \qquad\allowbreak \left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2},v=(v1​,v2​,…,vn​)∈Rn,∥v∥=v12​+⋯+vn2​​, is a vector in nnn-space, added, scaled and measured by the same formulas as before. We cannot draw Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn once nnn exceeds three, but the algebra is unchanged, and that is exactly what data science needs.

A record with nnn measured features—the pixels of an image, the word counts of a document, the readings of a sensor array—is a point of Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn, hence a vector. Their directions often matter more than their lengths (a document and its doubled copy should count as identical in subject), which is why the angle between two such vectors, introduced through the dot product in Section 2, becomes the standard cosine-similarity measure. The geometry of this unit is therefore not confined to two and three dimensions.

Linear combinations, independence and basis (intuition)

Adding scaled vectors, au+bv+cwa\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}+c\mathbf{w}au+bv+cw, gives a linear combination of them. Two questions recur throughout linear algebra: is a vector redundant—a combination of the others—and can a given set reach every vector?

A set of vectors is linearly independent if none of them is a linear combination of the rest—there is no redundancy. (Equivalently, and in the form the later Linear Algebra module uses: the only linear combination that equals 0\mathbf{0}0 is the one with all coefficients zero.) A basis is an independent set large enough to build every vector in the space, each in exactly one way. In R3\mathbb{R}^3R3 the standard i,j,k\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}i,j,k form the basic example: two vectors are independent unless they are parallel (counting 0\mathbf{0}0 as parallel to everything), and three are independent unless they lie in a common plane. In R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 two independent vectors already reach every vector, so three vectors in R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 are never independent: one of them is always a combination of the others. The systematic test for independence—and the general procedure that finds the coefficients of a combination—is the solution of a linear system by Gaussian elimination, which we develop in Unit 5. For now the geometric picture suffices.

In R2\mathbb{R}^2R2 the coefficients can already be found by hand, by matching components. To write (2,5)(2,5)(2,5) as α(4,1)+β(1,−2)\alpha(4,1)+\beta(1,-2)α(4,1)+β(1,−2), compare first and then second components: 4α+β=2,α−2β=5.4\alpha+\beta=2,\qquad\allowbreak \alpha-2\beta=5 .4α+β=2,α−2β=5. The first gives β=2−4α\beta=2-4\alphaβ=2−4α; substituting, α−2(2−4α)=5\alpha-2(2-4\alpha)=5α−2(2−4α)=5, so 9α=99\alpha=99α=9, giving α=1\alpha=1α=1 and β=−2\beta=-2β=−2. Check: (4,1)−2(1,−2)=(2,5)(4,1)-2(1,-2)=(2,5)(4,1)−2(1,−2)=(2,5). Because (4,1)(4,1)(4,1) and (1,−2)(1,-2)(1,−2) are not parallel, the same matching works for every target vector, so they form a basis of R2\mathbb{R}^2R2. By contrast (4,1)(4,1)(4,1) and (8,2)(8,2)(8,2) are parallel, hence dependent, and their combinations reach only the line through the origin that they share.

Products of vectors

There is no single “multiplication” of vectors. Two genuinely different products are useful: one returns a scalar and measures alignment; the other (in three dimensions) returns a vector perpendicular to its factors.

The scalar (dot) product

The dot product of u\mathbf{u}u and v\mathbf{v}v is the scalar u⋅v=u1v1+u2v2+u3v3(and u1v1+⋯+unvn in Rn).\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+u_3v_3 \qquad\allowbreak (\text{and } u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n \text{ in } \mathbb{R}^n).u⋅v=u1​v1​+u2​v2​+u3​v3​(and u1​v1​+⋯+un​vn​ in Rn).

The same number has a purely geometric description, and the link between the two is the whole reason the dot product is useful.

The angle between two vectors

Solving the geometric form for the cosine gives the formula that drives everything else.

The sign of u⋅v\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}u⋅v alone compares θ\thetaθ with a right angle: positive means θ<π/2\theta<\pi/2θ<π/2, zero means θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2θ=π/2 (a right angle), and negative means θ>π/2\theta>\pi/2θ>π/2. Orthogonality and projection

The dot product also resolves one vector along another. The projection of u\mathbf{u}u onto v\mathbf{v}v is the shadow u\mathbf{u}u casts on the line through v\mathbf{v}v (Figure 3); its signed length is the scalar projection ∥u∥cos⁡θ=u⋅v∥v∥\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|\cos\theta=\dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|}∥u∥cosθ=∥v∥u⋅v​.

The vector (cross) product

In R3\mathbb{R}^3R3, and only there, a second product returns a vector perpendicular to both factors.

Lines and planes

A point fixes where; a vector fixes which way. Together they pin down the two basic flat objects of space—the line and the plane—and the products of Section 2 turn those descriptions into equations.
