Unit 2

Complex numbers I: algebra and geometry

By the end of this unit you will be able to

  • Do complex arithmetic in Cartesian form using the conjugate and modulus.
  • Solve real quadratics with complex (conjugate-pair) roots.
  • Represent complex numbers on the Argand diagram and add them as vectors.
  • Convert between Cartesian and polar form; multiply and divide in polar form as rotation and scaling.

Unit 1’s chain of number systems cannot stop at the reals: x2+1=0x^2+1=0x2+1=0 has no real solution. This unit takes the final step, R⊂C\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}R⊂C. We do arithmetic with complex numbers, meet the conjugate and the modulus, picture everything on the Argand diagram, and finish with the polar form, in which multiplication becomes rotation and scaling.

Where this fits. The polar form leans on Unit 1’s trigonometry: radians, the values of sin⁡\sinsin and cos⁡\coscos, and the addition formulas. The geometric view of multiplication built here is the springboard for Unit 3: the exponential (Euler) form, De Moivre’s theorem and complex roots.

The algebra of complex numbers

The imaginary unit

To solve x2+1=0x^2+1=0x2+1=0 we need a number whose square is −1-1−1. No real number qualifies, so we introduce a new one.

The imaginary unit i\mathrm{i}i is a symbol obeying the single rule i 2=−1.\mathrm{i}^{\,2}=-1 .i2=−1. A complex number is an expression z=a+b iz=a+b\,\mathrm{i}z=a+bi with a,b∈Ra,b\in\mathbb{R}a,b∈R. The real number a=Re(z)a=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(z)a=Re(z) is the real part and b=Im(z)b=\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z)b=Im(z) is the imaginary part (itself a real number). The set of all complex numbers is denoted C\mathbb{C}C. Writing zzz as a+b ia+b\,\mathrm{i}a+bi is called its Cartesian form.

A real number is the special case b=0b=0b=0, so R⊂C\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}R⊂C; a number with a=0a=0a=0 and b≠0b\ne0b=0, such as 3i3\mathrm{i}3i, is called purely imaginary. Two complex numbers are equal exactly when they match in both parts.

a+b i=c+d ia+b\,\mathrm{i}=c+d\,\mathrm{i}a+bi=c+di if and only if a=ca=ca=c and b=db=db=d. A single complex equation thus encodes two real equations.

It is tempting to write i=−1\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}i=−1​, but this invites contradiction: blindly applying xy=xy\sqrt{x}\sqrt{y}=\sqrt{xy}x​y​=xy​ would give 1=1=(−1)(−1)=−1 −1=i⋅i=−11=\sqrt{1}=\sqrt{(-1)(-1)}=\sqrt{-1}\,\sqrt{-1}=\mathrm{i}\cdot\mathrm{i}=-11=1​=(−1)(−1)​=−1​−1​=i⋅i=−1. The rule xy=xy\sqrt{x}\sqrt{y}=\sqrt{xy}x​y​=xy​ holds only for non-negative reals. We therefore define i\mathrm{i}i by the property i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1 and never manipulate −1\sqrt{-1}−1​ directly.

Because i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1, the higher powers of i\mathrm{i}i cycle with period four. From i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1 we get i3=i2⋅i=−i\mathrm{i}^3=\mathrm{i}^2\cdot\mathrm{i}=-\mathrm{i}i3=i2⋅i=−i and i4=(i2)2=1\mathrm{i}^4=(\mathrm{i}^2)^2=1i4=(i2)2=1, after which the pattern repeats: i0=1,i1=i,i2=−1,i3=−i,i4=1,i5=i, …\mathrm{i}^0=1,\quad\allowbreak \mathrm{i}^1=\mathrm{i},\quad\allowbreak \mathrm{i}^2=-1,\quad\allowbreak \mathrm{i}^3=-\mathrm{i},\quad\allowbreak \mathrm{i}^4=1,\quad\allowbreak \mathrm{i}^5=\mathrm{i},\ \dotsi0=1,i1=i,i2=−1,i3=−i,i4=1,i5=i, …

To evaluate a power of i\mathrm{i}i, divide the exponent by 4 and keep only the remainder (this is called reducing it modulo 4). Since 2025=4⋅506+12025=4\cdot506+12025=4⋅506+1, i 2025=i 1=i.\mathrm{i}^{\,2025}=\mathrm{i}^{\,1}=\mathrm{i}.i2025=i1=i. Negative powers follow from i−1=1i=ii2=−i\mathrm{i}^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{i}}=\dfrac{\mathrm{i}}{\mathrm{i}^2}=-\mathrm{i}i−1=i1​=i2i​=−i.

Addition, subtraction and multiplication

We extend the arithmetic of R\mathbb{R}R to C\mathbb{C}C by requiring the familiar laws—commutativity, associativity and distributivity—to keep holding, together with the single new rule i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1. These requirements leave no freedom: they force the formulas below, which we therefore take as the definition of the three operations.

For z=a+b iz=a+b\,\mathrm{i}z=a+bi and w=c+d iw=c+d\,\mathrm{i}w=c+di, z+w=(a+c)+(b+d) i,z−w=(a−c)+(b−d) i,z w=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc) i.\begin{align*} z+w &= (a+c)+(b+d)\,\mathrm{i}, \\ z-w &= (a-c)+(b-d)\,\mathrm{i}, \\ z\,w &= (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)\,\mathrm{i}. \end{align*}z+wz−wzw​=(a+c)+(b+d)i,=(a−c)+(b−d)i,=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i.​

Equality of complex numbers turns one complex equation into two real ones. To find the real numbers x,yx,yx,y with (x+y i)(1+i)=3+i(x+y\,\mathrm{i})(1+\mathrm{i})=3+\mathrm{i}(x+yi)(1+i)=3+i, expand the left side: (x−y)+(x+y) i=3+i.(x-y)+(x+y)\,\mathrm{i}=3+\mathrm{i}.(x−y)+(x+y)i=3+i. The square of a complex number need not be a non-negative real number—indeed it need not be real at all. For example (2+3i)2=4+12i+9i2=−5+12i(2+3\mathrm{i})^2=4+12\mathrm{i}+9\mathrm{i}^2=-5+12\mathrm{i}(2+3i)2=4+12i+9i2=−5+12i. The complex conjugate

The complex conjugate of z=a+b iz=a+b\,\mathrm{i}z=a+bi is z‾=a−b i\overline{z}=a-b\,\mathrm{i}z=a−bi: the sign of the imaginary part is reversed.

Conjugation interacts cleanly with every operation. The following are checked by a one-line calculation each.

The Argand diagram

Complex numbers as points and vectors

Because a complex number z=a+b iz=a+b\,\mathrm{i}z=a+bi is determined by the real pair (a,b)(a,b)(a,b), it can be drawn as a point—or as the arrow from the origin to that point—in the plane. The horizontal axis carries the real part and the vertical axis the imaginary part; the plane used this way is the Argand diagram (or complex plane).

The picture immediately makes the modulus and the conjugate geometric: ∣z∣\left|z\right|∣z∣ is the length of the arrow (by Pythagoras), and z‾\overline{z}z is the reflection of zzz in the real axis. Likewise −z-z−z is the reflection of zzz through the origin.

Conjugation, modulus and the absence of order

The Argand diagram gives ∣z∣\left|z\right|∣z∣, z‾\overline{z}z and addition a geometric meaning. One familiar feature of the real numbers, however, is lost.

On the real line any two numbers satisfy exactly one of <,=,><,=,><,=,>, and the order obeys two rules that make it useful: adding the same number to both sides keeps an inequality, and the product of two positive numbers is positive. No ordering of C\mathbb{C}C can obey both. If one did, then any x≠0x\ne0x=0 would have x>0x>0x>0 or −x>0-x>0−x>0, and either way the second rule gives x2=(−x)2>0x^2=(-x)^2>0x2=(−x)2>0; applying this to x=ix=\mathrm{i}x=i gives i2=−1>0\mathrm{i}^2=-1>0i2=−1>0, while applying it to x=1x=1x=1 gives 1=12>01=1^2>01=12>0, i.e. −1<0-1<0−1<0. These contradict one another. So expressions like “z<wz<wz<w” are meaningless for non-real complex numbers—only the real quantities ∣z∣\left|z\right|∣z∣, Re(z)\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(z)Re(z), Im(z)\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z)Im(z) can be compared.
