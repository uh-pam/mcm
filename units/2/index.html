In Unit 1 we built the chain of number systems and noticed that it could not stop at the reals: the simple equation x2+1=0x^2+1=0x2+1=0 has no real solution, because the square of a real number is never negative. This unit takes the final step, R⊂C\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}R⊂C. We introduce the imaginary unit, do arithmetic with complex numbers, meet the conjugate and the modulus, picture everything on the Argand diagram, and finish with the polar form, in which multiplication becomes rotation and scaling.

Where this fits. This completes the number systems of Unit 1 and leans directly on its trigonometry: the unit circle, the values of sin⁡\sinsin and cos⁡\coscos, and the addition formulas all return when we develop the polar form. The geometric view of multiplication established here is the springboard for Unit 3, where the exponential (Euler) form, De Moivre’s theorem and complex roots make these ideas effortless.

The algebra of complex numbers

The imaginary unit

To solve x2+1=0x^2+1=0x2+1=0 we need a number whose square is −1-1−1. No real number qualifies, so we introduce a new one.

The imaginary unit i\mathrm{i}i is a symbol obeying the single rule i 2=−1.\mathrm{i}^{\,2}=-1 .i2=−1. A complex number is an expression z=a+b iz=a+b\,\mathrm{i}z=a+bi with a,b∈Ra,b\in\mathbb{R}a,b∈R. The set of all complex numbers is denoted C\mathbb{C}C.

Addition, subtraction and multiplication

We extend the arithmetic of R\mathbb{R}R to C\mathbb{C}C by requiring the familiar laws—commutativity, associativity and distributivity—to keep holding, together with the single new rule i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1. The complex conjugate

The Argand diagram

Complex numbers as points and vectors

Because a complex number z=a+b iz=a+b\,\mathrm{i}z=a+bi is determined by the real pair (a,b)(a,b)(a,b), it can be drawn as a point—or as the arrow from the origin to that point—in the plane. The horizontal axis carries the real part and the vertical axis the imaginary part; the plane used this way is the Argand diagram (or complex plane).

The picture immediately makes the modulus and the conjugate geometric: ∣z∣\left|z\right|∣z∣ is the length of the arrow (by Pythagoras), and z‾\overline{z}z is the reflection of zzz in the real axis. Likewise −z-z−z is the reflection of zzz through the origin.

Addition and subtraction as vectors

Since complex addition adds real and imaginary parts separately, it behaves exactly like vector addition in R2\mathbb{R}^2R2: z+wz+wz+w is found by the tip-to-tail (parallelogram) rule.

Subtraction has an equally useful reading: z−wz-wz−w is the vector from www to zzz, so ∣z−w∣\left|z-w\right|∣z−w∣ is the distance between the two points zzz and www in the plane. This turns many geometric statements into short algebraic ones.

For z=2+iz=2+\mathrm{i}z=2+i and w=1+3iw=1+3\mathrm{i}w=1+3i we have z+w=3+4iz+w=3+4\mathrm{i}z+w=3+4i, drawn above. The distance between the points is ∣z−w∣=∣(2−1)+(1−3)i∣=∣1−2i∣=12+(−2)2=5.\left|z-w\right|=\left|(2-1)+(1-3)\mathrm{i}\right|=\left|1-2\mathrm{i}\right|=\sqrt{1^2+(-2)^2}=\sqrt5 .∣z−w∣=∣(2−1)+(1−3)i∣=∣1−2i∣=12+(−2)2​=5​.

Identifying z=a+b iz=a+b\,\mathrm{i}z=a+bi with the point (a,b)(a,b)(a,b), the modulus ∣z−w∣\left|z-w\right|∣z−w∣ is exactly the Euclidean distance between two data points in the plane—the same distance that measures how alike two records are in machine learning. The plane-geometry of complex numbers is therefore the two-dimensional case of the Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn distance developed in Unit 4; multiplication, which we turn to next, adds a rotation that has no real-number counterpart and reappears as the phase of a signal in Unit 3.

Conjugation, modulus and the absence of order

Collecting the geometry: ∣z∣\left|z\right|∣z∣ is the distance from the origin, z‾\overline{z}z is the mirror image in the real axis (so ∣z‾∣=∣z∣\left|\overline{z}\right|=\left|z\right|∣z∣=∣z∣), and addition follows the parallelogram rule. One familiar feature of the real numbers, however, is lost.

On the real line any two numbers satisfy exactly one of <,=,><,=,><,=,>. No such ordering of C\mathbb{C}C is compatible with its arithmetic. If there were one, then for any x≠0x\ne0x=0 we would have x2>0x^2>0x2>0; applying this to x=ix=\mathrm{i}x=i gives i2=−1>0\mathrm{i}^2=-1>0i2=−1>0, while applying it to x=1x=1x=1 gives 1=12>01=1^2>01=12>0, i.e. −1<0-1<0−1<0. These contradict one another. So expressions like “z<wz<wz<w” are meaningless for non-real complex numbers—only the real quantities ∣z∣\left|z\right|∣z∣, Re(z)\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(z)Re(z), Im(z)\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z)Im(z) can be compared.

Why is i\mathrm{i}i defined by the property i2=−1\mathrm{i}^2=-1i2=−1 rather than by writing i=−1\mathrm{i}=\sqrt{-1}i=−1​?

The rule xy=xy\sqrt{x}\sqrt{y}=\sqrt{xy}x​y​=xy​ holds only for non-negative reals. Applied blindly it gives 1=1=(−1)(−1)=−1 −1=i⋅i=−11=\sqrt{1}=\sqrt{(-1)(-1)}=\sqrt{-1}\,\sqrt{-1}=\mathrm{i}\cdot\mathrm{i}=-11=1​=(−1)(−1)​=−1​−1​=i⋅i=−1, which is a genuine contradiction rather than a curiosity. Defining i\mathrm{i}i by the property it must satisfy means the symbol −1\sqrt{-1}−1​ never has to be manipulated at all. There is a second, quieter reason too: −1-1−1 has two square roots, ±i\pm\mathrm{i}±i, and no principled way to call one of them 'the' square root.

A student writes ∣3+4i∣=3+4=7|3+4\mathrm{i}|=3+4=7∣3+4i∣=3+4=7. What is the modulus, and why?

A cubic with real coefficients has 1+2i1+2\mathrm{i}1+2i among its roots. What follows immediately?
