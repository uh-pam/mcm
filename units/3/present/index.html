Unit 3

Complex numbers II: exponential form, De Moivre, roots

By the end of this unit you will be able to

  • Move between polar, exponential and trigonometric forms via Euler’s formula, and read eiθ\mathrm e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ as a rotation.
  • State, prove and apply De Moivre’s theorem for integer powers.
  • Solve wn=zw^n=zwn=z, interpret the roots of unity, and state the fundamental theorem of algebra.
  • Derive multiple-angle and trigonometric–hyperbolic identities by complex methods.
  • Add two same-frequency oscillations using phasors.

Unit 2 ended with a striking observation: multiplying complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles. This unit turns it into a tool. Euler’s formula packs the polar form into a single exponential eiθ\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ; De Moivre’s theorem then makes powers immediate; and from powers come nnnth roots, multiple-angle identities and a link to the hyperbolic functions.

Where this fits. We build on the polar form and multiplication rule of Unit 2 and the trigonometry of Unit 1. Euler’s formula we take on trust: its justification by power series belongs to the separate, later Calculus module (not Units 7–10). The exponential form recurs throughout the course and the sciences: oscillations, waves, AC circuits.

Euler’s formula and the exponential form

Euler’s formula

The polar form z=r(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)z=r(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)z=r(cosθ+isinθ) of Unit 2 has a remarkably compact rewriting. The complex exponential eiθ\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ has been given no prior meaning, so we are free to define it—and the definition that makes it behave like an exponential is Euler’s formula.

For every real θ\thetaθ, eiθ=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ.\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}=\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta .eiθ=cosθ+isinθ.

In that later Calculus module ez\mathrm{e}^{z}ez is instead defined by its power series, and Euler’s formula becomes a theorem, proved by comparing the series of ex\mathrm{e}^{x}ex, cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ and sin⁡θ\sin\thetasinθ. De Moivre’s theorem

Statement and proof

Raising a number in exponential form to a power is effortless, and the result, spelt out in trigonometric form, is De Moivre’s theorem.

For every integer nnn and every real θ\thetaθ, (cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)n=cos⁡nθ+isin⁡nθ.(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)^n=\cos n\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin n\theta .(cosθ+isinθ)n=cosnθ+isinnθ.

The proof uses mathematical induction, a way to prove a statement P(n)P(n)P(n) for every whole number n≥1n\ge1n≥1 in two steps. First show that P(1)P(1)P(1) is true (the base case). Then show that whenever P(k)P(k)P(k) is true, P(k+1)P(k+1)P(k+1) is true as well (the inductive step). Together these give P(1)P(1)P(1), hence P(2)P(2)P(2), hence P(3)P(3)P(3), and so on for every nnn, like a row of falling dominoes.

Proof. We argue by induction on n≥1n\ge 1n≥1. The base case n=1n=1n=1 is an identity. Assume the result holds for some n=k≥1n=k\ge 1n=k≥1. Then (cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)k+1=(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)k(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)=(cos⁡kθ+isin⁡kθ)(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ).(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)^{k+1} =(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)^{k}(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta) =(\cos k\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin k\theta)(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta).(cosθ+isinθ)k+1=(cosθ+isinθ)k(cosθ+isinθ)=(coskθ+isinkθ)(cosθ+isinθ). By the polar multiplication rule of Unit 2 (both factors have modulus 111, so we simply add the arguments), =cos⁡(kθ+θ)+isin⁡(kθ+θ)=cos⁡(k+1)θ+isin⁡(k+1)θ,=\cos(k\theta+\theta)+\mathrm{i}\sin(k\theta+\theta)=\cos(k+1)\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin(k+1)\theta,=cos(kθ+θ)+isin(kθ+θ)=cos(k+1)θ+isin(k+1)θ, which is the statement for n=k+1n=k+1n=k+1. By induction it holds for all n≥1n\ge1n≥1. It also holds for n=0n=0n=0 (both sides equal 111), and for negative integers n=−mn=-mn=−m: since (cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)−1=cos⁡θ−isin⁡θ=cos⁡(−θ)+isin⁡(−θ)(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)^{-1}=\cos\theta-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta=\cos(-\theta)+\mathrm{i}\sin(-\theta)(cosθ+isinθ)−1=cosθ−isinθ=cos(−θ)+isin(−θ), raising to the mmmth power and using the positive case gives cos⁡(−mθ)+isin⁡(−mθ)\cos(-m\theta)+\mathrm{i}\sin(-m\theta)cos(−mθ)+isin(−mθ). ◻

In exponential form the theorem is nothing more than the index law (eiθ)n=einθ,\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\bigr)^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta},(eiθ)n=einθ, which is precisely why the exponential notation is so powerful: De Moivre’s theorem is the statement that eiθ\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ obeys the usual rules of exponents.

The roots of unity

The most important special case takes z=1z=1z=1: the solutions of wn=1w^{n}=1wn=1 are the nnnth roots of unity.

The nnnth roots of unity are wk=ei2πk/n,k=0,1,…,n−1.w_{k}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi k/n}, \qquad\allowbreak k=0,1,\dots,n-1 .wk​=ei2πk/n,k=0,1,…,n−1. Writing ω=ei2π/n\omega=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi/n}ω=ei2π/n, they are the powers 1,ω,ω2,…,ω n−11,\omega,\omega^{2},\dots, \omega^{\,n-1}1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1: nnn equally spaced points on the unit circle, forming a regular nnn-gon with one vertex at 111.

For n≥2n\ge 2n≥2 the nnnth roots of unity sum to zero, 1+ω+ω2+⋯+ω n−1=01+\omega+\omega^{2}+\dots+\omega^{\,n-1}=01+ω+ω2+⋯+ωn−1=0.

With n=6n=6n=6 the roots are eiπk/3\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi k/3}eiπk/3 for k=0,…,5k=0,\dots,5k=0,…,5: 1,12+32i,−12+32i,−1,−12−32i,12−32i,1,\quad\allowbreak \tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\mathrm{i},\quad\allowbreak -\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\mathrm{i},\quad\allowbreak -1,\quad\allowbreak -\tfrac12-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\mathrm{i},\quad\allowbreak \tfrac12-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\mathrm{i},1,21​+23​​i,−21​+23​​i,−1,−21​−23​​i,21​−23​​i, the vertices of a regular hexagon. Notice they occur in conjugate pairs and include both real roots ±1\pm1±1.

The evenly spaced roots of unity are not just decorative. The discrete Fourier transform of nnn equally spaced samples of a signal combines them with powers of ω\omegaω as complex weights: the kkkth frequency it measures is the one that turns by ωk\omega^{k}ωk from each sample to the next. The symmetries of the roots of unity are what make the fast Fourier transform (FFT) fast, the algorithm data scientists and engineers use to analyse audio, images and time series. For the pure mathematician, the same set {1,ω,ω2,…,ω n−1}\{1,\omega,\omega^{2},\dots,\omega^{\,n-1}\}{1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1} is the simplest example of a cyclic group: every root is a power of the single generator ω\omegaω.

The fundamental theorem of algebra

The equation wn=zw^{n}=zwn=z solved in §3.1 is, after rearranging, the polynomial equation wn−z=0w^{n}-z=0wn−z=0 of degree nnn, and for z≠0z\ne0z=0 we found it has exactly nnn solutions in C\mathbb{C}C—no more and no fewer. This is the simplest case of the central fact about polynomials over the complex numbers.

A polynomial of degree n≥1n\ge1n≥1 with complex coefficients, p(w)=anwn+an−1wn−1+⋯+a1w+a0(an≠0),p(w)=a_{n}w^{n}+a_{n-1}w^{n-1}+\dots+a_{1}w+a_{0}\qquad\allowbreak (a_{n}\ne0),p(w)=an​wn+an−1​wn−1+⋯+a1​w+a0​(an​=0), has exactly nnn roots in C\mathbb{C}C, counted with multiplicity. Equivalently, it factors completely into linear factors, p(w)=an(w−w1)(w−w2)⋯(w−wn).p(w)=a_{n}(w-w_{1})(w-w_{2})\cdots(w-w_{n}).p(w)=an​(w−w1​)(w−w2​)⋯(w−wn​).

A genuine proof needs tools from the analysis side of mathematics and is beyond our scope. The theorem is the deepest reason the chain of number systems N⊂Z⊂Q⊂R⊂C\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}N⊂Z⊂Q⊂R⊂C stops at C\mathbb{C}C: unlike R\mathbb{R}R, the complex numbers are algebraically closed—no polynomial equation can lead outside them, so no further extension is ever needed to solve one. The nnnth roots of zzz above are one illustration; the conjugate-pair roots of the real quadratic in Unit 2 are another, reflecting the general rule that a polynomial with real coefficients has its non-real roots in conjugate pairs—the conjugate-pair theorem that Unit 2 proved for polynomials of every degree.

Putting the two theorems together gives a neat consequence. The non-real roots of a real polynomial come in conjugate pairs w,w‾w,\overline{w}w,w, so a real polynomial of odd degree, having an odd number of roots, must have at least one real one.

A link to the hyperbolic functions

Application: phasors and AC analysis

In oscillations and alternating-current circuits a sinusoid Acos⁡(ωt+φ)A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)Acos(ωt+φ) (here ω\omegaω is an angular frequency, no longer the root of unity of §3.3) is written as the real part of a rotating complex exponential, Acos⁡(ωt+φ)=Re ⁣(A eiφ eiωt)=Re ⁣(P eiωt),A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\!\bigl(A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\bigr) =\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\!\bigl(P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\bigr),Acos(ωt+φ)=Re(Aeiφeiωt)=Re(Peiωt), where the constant complex number P=A eiφP=A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}P=Aeiφ—the phasor—stores the amplitude and phase. For physicists the payoff is that, because differentiating in ttt merely multiplies eiωt\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}eiωt by iω\mathrm{i}\omegaiω, calculus with sinusoids becomes algebra with complex numbers.

The single most useful consequence is the painless addition of oscillations of the same frequency—sums that appear throughout AC circuit theory, wave interference and vibrations, and that are tedious to handle with trigonometric identities alone. For mmm oscillations with phasors P1,…,PmP_1,\dots,P_mP1​,…,Pm​: since the real part of a sum is the sum of the real parts, and every term carries the same factor eiωt\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}eiωt, ∑k=1mAkcos⁡(ωt+φk)=Re((∑k=1mPk)eiωt),\sum_{k=1}^{m} A_{k}\cos(\omega t+\varphi_{k}) =\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\Bigl(\bigl(\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}P_{k}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\Bigr),k=1∑m​Ak​cos(ωt+φk​)=Re((∑k=1m​Pk​)eiωt), so the result is another sinusoid of the same frequency, whose amplitude and phase are the modulus and argument of the phasor sum ∑k=1mPk\sum_{k=1}^{m}P_{k}∑k=1m​Pk​. (If the phasors sum to zero, as in a balanced three-phase supply, the oscillations cancel exactly.) That sum is just vector addition of the phasors in the Argand plane (Unit 2).
