Unit 2 ended with a striking observation: multiplying complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles. This unit turns that observation into a tool. Euler’s formula packs the polar form into a single exponential eiθ\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ; De Moivre’s theorem then makes powers immediate; and from powers we obtain the nnnth roots of any complex number, derive multiple-angle trigonometric identities, and glimpse the kinship between the trigonometric and hyperbolic functions.

Where this fits. We build directly on the polar form and the multiplication rule of Unit 2, and on the trigonometry of Unit 1. The one ingredient we take on trust is Euler’s formula itself, whose justification through power series belongs to the Calculus module; here we state it and put it to work. The exponential form and De Moivre’s theorem then recur throughout the course and across the sciences—oscillations, waves and AC circuits all speak this language.

Euler’s formula and the exponential form

Euler’s formula

The polar form z=r(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)z=r(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)z=r(cosθ+isinθ) of Unit 2 has a remarkably compact rewriting. The complex exponential eiθ\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ has been given no prior meaning, so we are free to define it—and the definition that makes it behave like an exponential is Euler’s formula.

For every real θ\thetaθ, eiθ=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ.\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}=\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta .eiθ=cosθ+isinθ.

In the Calculus module ez\mathrm{e}^{z}ez is instead defined by its power series, and Euler’s formula becomes a theorem, proved by comparing the series of ex\mathrm{e}^{x}ex, cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ and sin⁡θ\sin\thetasinθ. Here we have no such machinery, so we take the formula itself as the definition; the checks below confirm it behaves exactly as an exponential should. (For a general exponent, ea+ib:=ea(cos⁡b+isin⁡b)\mathrm{e}^{a+\mathrm{i}b}:=\mathrm{e}^{a}(\cos b+\mathrm{i}\sin b)ea+ib:=ea(cosb+isinb).)

eiθ\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ as a rotation

Because ∣eiα∣=1\left|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}\right|=1​eiα​=1, multiplying any complex number zzz by eiα\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\alpha}eiα leaves its modulus unchanged and increases its argument by α\alphaα. Sine and cosine as exponentials

Euler’s formula can be run backwards. Writing it for θ\thetaθ and for −θ-\theta−θ, eiθ=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ,e−iθ=cos⁡θ−isin⁡θ,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}=\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta, \qquad \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}=\cos\theta-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta,eiθ=cosθ+isinθ,e−iθ=cosθ−isinθ, and then adding and subtracting gives the inverse Euler formulas.

cos⁡θ=eiθ+e−iθ2,sin⁡θ=eiθ−e−iθ2i.\cos\theta=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}}{2\mathrm{i}}.cosθ=2eiθ+e−iθ​,sinθ=2ieiθ−e−iθ​.

These convert products and powers of sines and cosines into sums of exponentials, which is exactly what is needed both for the multiple-angle identities of Section 3.3 and, later, for integration in the Calculus module.

De Moivre’s theorem

Statement and proof

Raising a number in exponential form to a power is effortless, and the result, spelt out in trigonometric form, is De Moivre’s theorem.

For every integer nnn and every real θ\thetaθ, (cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)n=cos⁡nθ+isin⁡nθ.(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)^n=\cos n\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin n\theta .(cosθ+isinθ)n=cosnθ+isinnθ.

Proof. We argue by induction on n≥1n\ge 1n≥1. The base case n=1n=1n=1 is an identity. Assume the result holds for some n=k≥1n=k\ge 1n=k≥1. Then (cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)k+1=(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)k(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ)=(cos⁡kθ+isin⁡kθ)(cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ).(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)^{k+1} =(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta)^{k}(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta) =(\cos k\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin k\theta)(\cos\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin\theta).(cosθ+isinθ)k+1=(cosθ+isinθ)k(cosθ+isinθ)=(coskθ+isinkθ)(cosθ+isinθ). By the polar multiplication rule of Unit 2 (both factors have modulus 111, so we simply add the arguments), =cos⁡(kθ+θ)+isin⁡(kθ+θ)=cos⁡(k+1)θ+isin⁡(k+1)θ,=\cos(k\theta+\theta)+\mathrm{i}\sin(k\theta+\theta)=\cos(k+1)\theta+\mathrm{i}\sin(k+1)\theta,=cos(kθ+θ)+isin(kθ+θ)=cos(k+1)θ+isin(k+1)θ, which is the statement for n=k+1n=k+1n=k+1. In exponential form the theorem is nothing more than the index law (eiθ)n=einθ,\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\bigr)^{n}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta},(eiθ)n=einθ, which is precisely why the exponential notation is so powerful: De Moivre’s theorem is the statement that eiθ\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}eiθ obeys the usual rules of exponents.

The most important special case takes z=1z=1z=1: the solutions of wn=1w^{n}=1wn=1 are the nnnth roots of unity.

The nnnth roots of unity are wk=ei2πk/n,k=0,1,…,n−1.w_{k}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi k/n}, \qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1 .wk​=ei2πk/n,k=0,1,…,n−1. The evenly spaced roots of unity are not just decorative. Sampling a signal at the nnnth roots of unity is the basis of the discrete Fourier transform—the powers ωk\omega^{k}ωk are exactly the frequencies a length-nnn DFT measures, and the fast algorithm (the FFT) that data scientists and engineers use to analyse audio, images and time series rests on their arithmetic. For the pure mathematician, the same set {1,ω,ω2,…,ω n−1}\{1,\omega,\omega^{2},\dots,\omega^{\,n-1}\}{1,ω,ω2,…,ωn−1} is the simplest example of a cyclic group: every root is a power of the single generator ω\omegaω.

The fundamental theorem of algebra

The equation wn=zw^{n}=zwn=z solved in §3.1 is, after rearranging, the polynomial equation wn−z=0w^{n}-z=0wn−z=0 of degree nnn, and we found it has exactly nnn solutions in C\mathbb{C}C—no more and no fewer. This is the simplest case of the central fact about polynomials over the complex numbers.

A polynomial of degree n≥1n\ge1n≥1 with complex coefficients, p(w)=anwn+an−1wn−1+⋯+a1w+a0(an≠0),p(w)=a_{n}w^{n}+a_{n-1}w^{n-1}+\dots+a_{1}w+a_{0}\qquad(a_{n}\ne0),p(w)=an​wn+an−1​wn−1+⋯+a1​w+a0​(an​=0), has exactly nnn roots in C\mathbb{C}C, counted with multiplicity. Equivalently, it factors completely into linear factors, p(w)=an(w−w1)(w−w2)⋯(w−wn).p(w)=a_{n}(w-w_{1})(w-w_{2})\cdots(w-w_{n}).p(w)=an​(w−w1​)(w−w2​)⋯(w−wn​).

A genuine proof needs tools from the analysis side of mathematics and is beyond our scope. The theorem is the deepest reason the chain of number systems N⊂Z⊂Q⊂R⊂C\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}N⊂Z⊂Q⊂R⊂C stops at C\mathbb{C}C: unlike R\mathbb{R}R, the complex numbers are algebraically closed—no polynomial equation can lead outside them, so no further extension is ever needed. The nnnth roots of zzz above are one illustration; the conjugate-pair roots of the real quadratic in Unit 2 are another, reflecting the general rule that a polynomial with real coefficients has its non-real roots in conjugate pairs. That rule is worth proving, since it costs only the conjugation algebra of Unit 2.

If p(w)=0p(w)=0p(w)=0 then p(w‾)=0‾=0p(\overline{w})=\overline{0}=0p(w)=0=0, so w‾\overline{w}w is a root too. ◻

Thus the non-real roots of a real polynomial come in conjugate pairs w,w‾w,\overline{w}w,w; in particular a real polynomial of odd degree, having an odd number of roots, must have at least one real one. This is exactly the conjugate pair seen for the real quadratic of Unit 2, now established for any degree.

A link to the hyperbolic functions

The inverse Euler formulas also explain the family resemblance, noted in Unit 1, between the trigonometric and hyperbolic functions: each pair is the other viewed along the imaginary direction in C\mathbb{C}C. Feeding an imaginary argument into cos⁡\coscos and sin⁡\sinsin, cos⁡(ix)=ei(ix)+e−i(ix)2=e−x+ex2=cosh⁡x,\cos(\mathrm{i}x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{i}x)}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{i}x)}}{2}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}+\mathrm{e}^{x}}{2}=\cosh x,cos(ix)=2ei(ix)+e−i(ix)​=2e−x+ex​=coshx, sin⁡(ix)=ei(ix)−e−i(ix)2i=e−x−ex2i=−ex−e−x2i=isinh⁡x,\sin(\mathrm{i}x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{i}x)}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{i}x)}}{2\mathrm{i}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-x}-\mathrm{e}^{x}}{2\mathrm{i}} =-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{x}-\mathrm{e}^{-x}}{2\mathrm{i}}=\mathrm{i}\sinh x,sin(ix)=2iei(ix)−e−i(ix)​=2ie−x−ex​=−2iex−e−x​=isinhx, the last step using −1/i=i-1/\mathrm{i}=\mathrm{i}−1/i=i. Application: phasors and AC analysis

In oscillations and alternating-current circuits a sinusoid Acos⁡(ωt+φ)A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)Acos(ωt+φ) is written as the real part of a rotating complex exponential, Acos⁡(ωt+φ)=Re ⁣(A eiφ eiωt)=Re ⁣(P eiωt),A\cos(\omega t+\varphi)=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\!\bigl(A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\bigr) =\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\!\bigl(P\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\bigr),Acos(ωt+φ)=Re(Aeiφeiωt)=Re(Peiωt), where the constant complex number P=A eiφP=A\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi}P=Aeiφ—the phasor—stores the amplitude and phase. Because differentiating in ttt merely multiplies eiωt\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}eiωt by iω\mathrm{i}\omegaiω, calculus with sinusoids becomes algebra with complex numbers.

The single most useful consequence is the painless addition of oscillations of the same frequency—sums that appear throughout AC circuit theory, wave interference and vibrations, and that are tedious to handle with trigonometric identities alone. Since Re\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}Re is linear and every term carries the same factor eiωt\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}eiωt, ∑kAkcos⁡(ωt+φk)=Re((∑kPk)eiωt),\sum_{k} A_{k}\cos(\omega t+\varphi_{k}) =\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\Bigl(\bigl(\textstyle\sum_{k}P_{k}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\Bigr),k∑​Ak​cos(ωt+φk​)=Re((∑k​Pk​)eiωt), so the result is another sinusoid of the same frequency, whose amplitude and phase are the modulus and argument of the phasor sum ∑kPk\sum_{k}P_{k}∑k​Pk​. That sum is just vector addition of the phasors in the Argand plane (Unit 2).
